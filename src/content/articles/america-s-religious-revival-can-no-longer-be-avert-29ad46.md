# America’s religious revival can no longer be averted

*The young are poor: Part III*

America is primed for its next great religious revival.

The conditions are set — just as they were in 1739, on the eve of George Whitefield’s great tour of the colonies. Just as they were in 1800, the year before twenty thousand gathered for the camp meeting at Cane Ridge. And just as they were in 1948, the year before Billy Graham’s Los Angeles crusade made him a national figure.

Only the return of aggressive, confident, surging devotion can serve as the foundation of the social capital that our young need. As we saw in Part II, the evidence is overwhelming: there has never been a substitute for faith when it comes to building true bonds.

America has always been a country of religious revolution. The pattern is cyclical: sects establish themselves, at first radical and demanding, as were the Puritans and the Quakers. These sects mature into denominations and gain institutional power. Gradually, the monopolies they establish ossify, and their prelates become too comfortable. Their need to inspire declines in proportion to their political and financial power.

For a time, they are able to maintain the appearance of dominance and vitality, drawing on state subsidies and the wealth of aging congregations. They may even succeed in establishing regulatory monopolies, as the Congregationalists did in New England until well into the nineteenth century. It is these state monopolies which have proved so fatal to the vitality of the churches of Europe.

On the fringes, radical forces begin to stir. New sects and orders summon tremendous energy by demanding a return to a more radical faith, imposing strict doctrines, rejecting the profane, and turning to otherworldliness. For a time, the upstarts can be contained to the frontier, but geriatric organizations can only hold them at bay for so long.

Ultimately, the cycle reaches its crescendo when the monopolies are broken, and the radical sects — who by this point have found their heroic figures and mastered their ceremonies — explode into the mainstream, capturing huge parts of the population in the decades which follow.

Mainstream commentary about these ‘religious revivals’ implies that they are best explained by changes in ‘demand’ for religion due to changing socio-economic conditions. But I disagree: man is a religious animal, and the demand is constant.

What changes is supply. The research of Rodney Stark and Roger Finke is important here. Every era offers its citizens a different faith. This is a hopeful finding, and a demanding one. Revival comes to those who build it.

If demand is constant, we should find that the past half-century produced a revival of its own. It did. Progressivism was the great religious movement of our age.

Progressivism had sin and purity, saints and heretics, rites of public confession, and an evangelistic zeal that carried it through every institution in the Western world. Few recognized it as a true religion, just as eighteenth-century Anglicans saw in Methodism only a disorder of enthusiasm. But a religion it was.

That pseudo-revival is now spent. Few of its causes still inspire genuine devotion. Our question, then, is why no theologically conservative movement has yet emerged to take its place.

The religious market was never as free as it appeared. Finke and Stark show that the upstarts flourished only as the regulation and suppression of their religion finally fell away. In the colonies, the established churches were quite happy to wield the full force of the law to maintain position.

Virginia’s Anglican establishment jailed Baptist preachers for preaching without a licence. In New England, Congregational churches were organized at town meetings and supported by compulsory religious taxes. Dissenters who refused to pay saw their property seized.

Our own age has an establishment of its own. It is sprawling and insidious, sometimes visible and sometimes hidden, sometimes admitted and sometimes denied. The power of this establishment is written into the law, into propaganda, and sometimes directly into the hearts of its children. Neema Parvini calls it the Boomer Truth Regime. In his essay of that name, he describes a generation:

> “…animated by a mythic worldview in which progress necessitates the tearing down of all boundaries, hierarchies, systems of belief, restraints, and controls upon the ultimate good of self-expression.”

Parvini insists that this worldview be understood as myth, and myth in a strong sense:

> “A myth is deeper than mere narrative, it is something that prefigures discourse and operates on a level ‘underneath the mind.’ In every society, in every epoch, a hegemonic mode of thought will be all-pervasive such that its non-adherents will be branded as heretics, pariahs, or madmen. This is something stronger than mere ideology or worldview; it is rather an almost hermetically sealed system of a priori assumptions and values that govern how men and women relate to the world around them and to their history and culture. This we call a ‘truth regime.’”

Any movement that preaches boundaries, hierarchy, restraint and duty, which is to say any serious Christianity, stood condemned under such a regime as heresy. Its suppression is partly de jure. Hate speech laws, inclusion rules and equality regulations have put Christian orthodoxy at legal risk across the Western world.

The suppression is also de facto. Conservative Christians have been quietly marginalized from the spaces leaders are made and culture is formed: elite universities, newsrooms, film studios, philanthropic foundations and regulatory bodies.

Tocqueville, visiting America in the 1830s, concluded that “any alliance with any political power whatsoever is bound to be burdensome for religion.” The established churches of our time have made peace with the regime, and so they have paid the same price the colonial establishments paid: irrelevance followed by collapse.

Unbelief in American intellectual life was formulated in the divinity schools as much as in the science departments. Miracles were treated as illustrative fiction rather than historical fact. The religious wanted to lose God in order to better make company with the rising progressives. Stark and Finke cite the historian James Turner, who concluded that “the defenders of God slowly strangled Him.”

When upstarts appeared within the old churches, the centre moved against them. The Congregationalist establishment of the 1820s opposed Charles Finney’s hugely successful frontier revivals, preferring an intellectualized and “reasonable” religion whatever the cost in converts. Finke and Stark suggest that had they embraced Finney, the Congregationalists might have towered over the nation by 1850.

The Catholic Church has always channelled its sectarian energies into religious orders and kept them within the fold. Yet when young traditionalist communities began to fill the Latin Masses, Rome moved to restrain them, as with the restrictions of ‘Traditionis Custodes’ in 2021. The Boomer Truth Regime was strong enough to challenge Church norms established over millennia.

The regime’s grip was strong enough that even those who saw past it stepped back from the edge. Jordan Peterson told a generation of young men to shoulder the heaviest responsibility they could bear, and millions listened and responded as best they could.

But he could never bring himself to tell them where to carry it. He did not ask them to join a church, profess a creed or bind themselves to a body, and he always hedged questions of otherwordliness. The relationship he built was simplex, to use the terminology of the previous essay: one speaker, a vast audience, and no bonds running between its members or back to him. An enormous reserve of moral energy was raised and then dispersed.

A man who sensed the great reserve of latent religious demand and hunger for otherworldliness fell prey to his regime conditioning. But the energy he discovered still lies dormant under the surface of society, waiting to be summoned again — by someone who truly wants it.

Oswald Spengler predicted in ‘The Decline of the West’ that late civilizations would undergo a ‘Second Religiousness’: a return to faith that is passive, syncretic and consoling. This is Christianity as a refuge for the exhausted rather than a calling for the animated. Peterson is its clearest modern example of this false-revival. He offered the consolations of Christianity, its stories, its symbols and an attempt at its moral seriousness, without offering Christ Himself.

Millions took the consolation, while few found a true church. But they’re still out there, looking. Hungry.

Established religions, in the words of Stark and Finke, “shift their emphasis toward this world and away from the next,” easing the tension between themselves and their surroundings. Members who want a stronger faith grow discontented, and in time they leave to found something stricter. “The result is an endless cycle of sect formation, transformation, schism, and rebirth.”

The otherworldly turn strips the shell of the world away. It lets what is most essentially true about the faith shine through, the creed, the sacraments and the moral law, free of the accretions of a dead age. That raw faith can then join with cultural forms suited to the present. Augustine addressed God as a beauty “so ancient and so new.”

The regime that hardened this shell, and shaped the faith of men like Peterson, is now failing. Louise Perry notes in ‘Boomer Christianity Is Dying Fast’ that the average American Catholic bishop is sixty-five and a half. The boomers who lead the churches grew up in a high-trust, religious, family-centered culture, and then enjoyed its demolition in the 1960s. Their energy relies on burning a fuel source which is largely spent. “This is a trick that can only be pulled once.”

Secularization has meanwhile boiled off the most progressive believers, leaving the churches “smaller, more devout and more conservative.” Young Christians now skew male, and young priests are far more conservative than old ones.

In such times, a smaller church is not necessarily a cause for despair. Finke and Stark found that American Catholicism grew so vigorous precisely because it was a minority. The Church, they argue, generates intense participation “only where the Catholic Church is in the minority and is somewhat embattled.”

Christians are becoming a minority in the nations their faith built, which is the condition in which they have historically been strongest. That the Catholic faith in America had to live ‘in the arena’ is much of the reason it is vital today while the faith slumbers in much of Europe.

The upstarts are growing in strength and confidence, while the regime weakens. It is only a matter of time before a hero arises and they break through.

If religion is the key to social capital, and social capital is the only remaining pathway for the young to achieve the pillars of the good life — education, employment marriage, mortgage, children — then what steps must we take next?

Social capital is made by people who cannot get away from one another. Trust, reputation, obligation and honor are the fruits of involuntary recurrence, of the same men meeting again and again whether they like it or not. As we saw, the twentieth century abolished the involuntary.

Our question, then, is ‘how does a free people bind itself?’

History offers one reliable guarantor of such promises. The oath of the man of faith.

Robert Putnam — no great partisan of religion — called faith communities in which people worship together “arguably the single most important repository of social capital in America.” Nearly half of all associational memberships in America were church-related, half of all personal philanthropy was religious in character, and half of all volunteering took place in a religious context.

There is no road back to a thick social fabric except through the church.

The fourteenth-century historian Ibn Khaldun knew this. His Bedouins were wild and free: brave, self-reliant, and almost impossible to unite. They were “rude, proud, ambitious, and eager to be the leaders. Their individual aspirations rarely coincide.”

No chief could weld them together for long. But religion could. When a prophet or saint called upon them “to fulfil the commands of God,” their “haughtiness and jealousy” left them, and “it is, then, easy for them to subordinate themselves and to unite”.

The young men now turning toward the faith resemble his Bedouins: independent and combative. They will not submit to an intellectual. They will — if properly inspired — submit to God, and in submitting to Him they become able to integrate with one another.

Part II described how mid-century families fled the cities for the suburbs in search of physical security, and how that flight became the largest replacement purchase in American history. Today a different migration is under way. Families are moving to live near strong parishes and congregations, in search of spiritual security.

This is largely opaque to the outside world, but once you start moving in these communities, you realize the energy that these families are summoning is startling.

Both the move to the suburb and the move to the religious community are ultimately animated by the safety of children. The suburban family sought mobility and distance. But the parish family moves in order to join. It moves for contact. It moves for spiritual security.

Children are both the trigger for these moves and the test of those who make them. In such communities, “how many children do you have?” is a common question. It is loaded: a test. A family that has reached a certain age with fewer than three, and ideally four, may not be serious enough to belong fully.

In ‘Why Strict Churches Are Strong’, Laurence Iannaccone observed that any group producing shared goods suffers from free riders, who take the benefits while others bear the costs. Strict religious groups solve the problem by demanding high costs of everyone, so that potential members “are forced to choose whether to participate fully or not at all.” Only the radical survive.

Children are expensive and difficult in modernity. This is why a large family is a credible signal. It shows that the parents obey a command higher than the world’s, and that they will make good neighbors with whom to raise the next generation. “As arrows are in the hand of a mighty man; so are children of the youth. Happy is the man that hath his quiver full of them” (Psalm 127).

These communities, in which true social fabric is re-asserting itself, have certain commonalities.

The first is worship at the centre. Communities founded for the sake of community tend to fail, because community is a poor thing to worship. As C. S. Lewis put it in ‘Mere Christianity’: “Aim at Heaven and you will get earth ‘thrown in’: aim at earth and you will get neither.” The biblical mandate: “Seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you” (Matthew 6:33).

The second is function. In ‘The Quest for Community’, Robert Nisbet explains why the old institutions held men’s loyalty, and why they lost it:

> “Family, church, local community drew and held the allegiances of individuals in earlier times not because of any superior impulses to love and protect, or because of any greater natural harmony of intellectual and spiritual values, or even because of any superior internal organization, but because these groups possessed a virtually indispensable relation to the economic and political order.”

Birth and death, courtship and marriage, unemployment, sickness and old age were all met through these groups. When the state and the market usurped them, it was inevitable that allegiance would the functions.

The immigrant parishes understood this. After 1880 the parish became a community institution, and above it the Church built “a kind of parallel Catholic America” of schools, hospitals, charities and societies.

The new communities are doing the same: education through Christian schools and homeschooling co-operatives and work through the community’s networks. Patronage secures allegiance.

The third is cost. The anthropologist Richard Sosis, studying nineteenth-century American communes, found that religious communes long outlasted secular ones. Among the religious, the more a commune demanded of its members, the longer it survived.

Ignazio Silone, recalling his years in the Communist Party, described the same mechanism from inside his twisted faith: the links that bound its members “grew steadily firmer, not in spite of the dangers and sacrifices involved, but because of them.” He added a sentence every youth pastor of the boomer era should have read:

> “Anyone who thinks he can wean the best and most serious-minded young people away from communism by enticing them into a well-warmed hall to play billiards, starts from an extremely limited and unintelligent conception of mankind.”

One example of a productive cost that lives acutely in my own mind is banning the screen. My child has no phone, but there’s a five-year old at the secular school he attends with TikTok (we’re in the process of pulling him out).

No family can solve the screen alone, because a child without a phone among children with phones is merely isolated. It is a collective action problem. The religious are among the best placed to solve it.

In Cork in 1838, Father Theobald Mathew began administering a public pledge of total abstinence. Several million Irishmen took it. A similar, informal pledge is quietly being made about childhood screens in certain communities across America. There is no question that if you send your child off to play at their house, you need be concerned about screens and the internet.

The fourth is proximity. Multiplexity demands that worship, school, and work share a place, ideally a neighborhood walkable from the church door. Through this closeness, friendships, marriages, and memory form. Life in close quarters makes kin of strangers.

The fifth is children. Winthrop’s company sailed so “that we may live and be multiplied.” In a dense, devout community, young men and women grow up knowing one another’s families, character and prospects, and the otherwise brutal search happens naturally.

A community that meets these conditions will eventually grow rich in ways the statistics do not record. If the young are poor because they must buy at retail what their grandparents inherited in the commons, the reverse also holds.

A family embedded in a dense, devout community freely receives everything that we have otherwise lost: childcare from kin and neighbors, high-trust relationships, recommendations for work, and good marriages for its children. Social capital is capital. Fanaticism becomes a pathway to wealth.

The hardship of the young is a surmountable obstacle. Ibn Khaldun wrote that “the desert is the basis and reservoir of civilization and cities.” Group feeling is forged where life is hard and decays where it is easy. The generation that inherited the commons spent it. The generation that inherited nothing must build it again.

How does a free people bind itself? Winthrop answered aboard the Arbella:

> “We are entered into covenant with him for this work”.

Free men drew up their own terms. From that day God would “expect a strict performance of the articles contained in it.” Their freedom was spent on a covenant, and the covenant made them a people.

They would be “as a city upon a hill, the eyes of all people are upon us,” and if they failed, they would be “made a story and a by-word through the world.”

The eyes of all people will be on the new communities too. Winthrop ended his sermon with the words of Moses:

> “Therefore let us choose life, that we, and our seed, may live.”

We must choose the same.

Sic transit imperium,
