# Childhood freedom is a now luxury good

*Inside the rich and radical islands of functional families*

*I will be giving a private talk next week in San Francisco. We have a few spaces left. If you’d like to come, reply to this email with a sentence or two about who you are and why you’re interested. — Johann*

* * *

Giving children the freedom they need to develop agency is now a luxury good. The number of neighborhoods in which it is normal for children to leave the house and roam all day has collapsed. This collapse has come for a variety of reasons relating to security, trust, law, norms, and infrastructure.

Allowing children the privilege of freedom depends on conditions that most families no longer have access to: safe streets - yes - but also neighbors who are known and trusted, and a settled local agreement about what children are and what they are for. These conditions have not vanished, but they have concentrated, and are now a guarded secret, found only in private, privileged, and intentional communities.

This is a curious inversion of an older pattern. For most of history the peasant’s son had the run of the village while the noble’s son was kept under tutors. Now it is the wealthy child who is sent out to enjoy the freedom and adventure of camps and screenless schools, while working and underclass children are kept indoors and screened up.

*[image]*

It is worth being clear about the factors which underlie this transition. Otherwise, parents seeking the nostalgic ‘free roaming’ experience are directed to explanations which are emphasized because they are unproblematic and suggest that a broad solution is available if we *just move policy in a sensible direction*. This includes discussions of ‘walkable development’ and a rejection of ‘helicopter parenting’.

This polite framing avoids the reality that the prudent decisions available to parents are mostly made for them by the place they can afford to live, the people they live among, and how radical they are willing to be.

Children develop *‘agency’* - the self-belief that they can independently and effectively manipulate and shape the world in creative ways - through constant experimentation and positive reinforcement.

The ‘independent’ aspect of this formula involves developing internal psychological permission to break from prosaic norms and routines. Developing this is helped by play outside the control of authorities and interacting with the real world in settings unmediated by parents.

The closed systems that now fill children’s hours provide some feeling of agency (open world games, sprawling social media platforms, private chat rooms) without its substance. A child scrolling or playing through the programmatic logic of games is making choices, but they are only the choices that limited systems can accommodate.

Closed-system childhoods teach that there are inviolable hidden structures underneath reality and that the smoothest and most rewarding experiences are to be found when you conform with them. *Experiences from boxes teach you to think within boxes.* And the vice available online can be as controlling as any parent.

In 1971 the Policy Studies Institute in London surveyed how English children moved about. Roughly eighty per cent of seven- and eight-year-olds were allowed to travel to school without an adult. By 1990, when Mayer Hillman, John Adams and John Whitelegg repeated the study and published it as *One False Move*, that share had fallen to about nine per cent. Inside a single generation an everyday liberty had disappeared.

The obvious explanation is that the world grew more dangerous and parents were forced to respond. But it did not; at least not in an obvious, total sense. Violent crime in the United States fell by half from the early 1990s, across the decades in which supervision tightened hardest. The collapse of the old childhood did not track macro societal danger.

Free childhoods disappeared alongside the loss of something harder to measure: the trust that makes a child outdoors seem normal rather than negligent and the local conditions that once made such trust reasonable. These conditions - like many other kinds of social integrity such as intact two-parent homes - have become aggressively sorted by class.

The first necessary condition for freedom is bonds among neighbors. The sociologist Robert Sampson, studying Chicago neighborhoods, named the quality that keeps a place orderly and safe without constant policing. He called it ‘*collective efficacy’*: the mix of mutual trust among residents and their willingness, as he put it, *“to intervene on behalf of the common good.”*

His researchers measured it by asking: *would your neighbors do something if children were skipping school and making trouble on the corner?*

Where the answer was yes, violence was lower, regardless of how poor the neighborhood was. Collective efficacy is the thing a parent relies on when he lets a young child out of sight. It is the assumption that if the child falls, or gets lost or frightened, the nearest adult will recognize the situation, know what to do, and act as the parent would want. This assumes a coherent understanding of the *‘common good’.*

Migration matters. Sampson found collective efficacy strongest where residents owned their homes and stayed put, and weakest where the population churned. Robert Putnam’s well-known study *“E Pluribus Unum”* added the uncomfortable finding that in more ethnically diverse neighborhoods, residents of every background tend to *“hunker down”*: trust falls, even trust of one’s own group, and people withdraw.

A parent in a stable and homogeneous place can predict how the adults around him will behave toward his child. A parent in a fragmented one cannot and naturally keeps children close.

* * *

Lane Scott described the collapse of neighborhood trust well in a note that I haven’t been able to stop thinking about since I read it:

> *The Stranger Things model of childhood where parents were mostly completely in the dark about what those kids ages 10-18 were doing most of the time, but knew they were with their friends and all the moms just called each other and said “hey have you seen my kid?” when they needed to find them is the correct way to raise children. We just cannot do that in a society that allows bad people with bad intentions free access to kids, and at least half the adults are on another planet when it comes to morality and basic civilizational stability.*

>
> *So we don’t even let kids go outside unattended, and we spend all our extra cash on “activities” to get them out of the house and into the world, and then we wonder why our grown kids can’t figure out dating and friend groups, and we wonder why people have fewer and fewer kids.*

The childhood depicted in nostalgic media rested on a dense web of adults who knew each other, shared a rough moral sense, and could be relied upon to mind each other’s children. That web is now a feature of particular places (often expensive places) rather than a general inheritance, and discerning which places have kept it is of key importance for families hoping to raise agentic children with deep networks of trusted friends.

Perversely, this turns ‘overpriced homes’ into a feature, rather than a bug. When families have no organic access to likeminded, competent, stable, trustworthy families, they use one of the only mechanisms left which forces some kind of homogeneity: *money*. People’s revealed preference is to live in arbitrarily expensive neighborhoods because the cost is a moat against endemic dysfunction. The second condition of the ‘childhood agency as luxury good’ development is community relationship with screens. The affluent now pay to keep their children off screens while everyone else’s children are quietly handed over to the tyranny of the virtual. Silicon Valley’s elite Waldorf School is screen free, for example. Human contact is increasingly a privilege.

A screen is the cheapest available childcare and the most effective. To refuse it, a parent needs time, attention, money for alternatives, a spouse or community to share the load, and the stubbornness to hold a line that the surrounding culture is pushing against.

But wealth alone is insufficient. To give a personal example, by far our largest household expenditure is the private school that we send our eldest son to. One day when I picked him up, I was blown away to see a five-year-old scrolling TikTok in the playground, with other boys gravitating around him to catch a glimpse. We need a more radical community.

Norms and beliefs matter. Ave Maria, in southwest Florida, was built by Tom Monaghan, the founder of Domino’s Pizza, as a town laid out for Catholic families. Its whole plan oriented around a large central church. Children ride their bicycles to the parish school and families walk to the shops, and the place fills in the evenings with something close to the street life that has drained out of ordinary suburbs. By 2023 it was the fastest-growing new community in the United States.

In ‘normal’ communities, by contrast, a parent who wishes to give his child an older form of freedom now risks being reported and investigated for it. When Lenore Skenazy let her nine-year-old ride the New York subway alone in 2008 and wrote about it, she was branded America’s worst mother and coined, in self-defense, the term “free-range kids.” Similar examples from middle class suburbs have since arisen.

The communities which escape this paranoia occupy a barbell distribution. At the low end, they’re totally dysfunctional and actual abuse and neglect is so rife that authorities have bigger problems than kids walking about unsupervised.

At the other end of the distribution, they’re notably wealthy and/or religiously radical. In these communities, security is better assured and successful parents are extended the presumption of competence by their neighbors and the state.

Wealthy communities also perpetuate childhood freedom in private, walled-off spaces via boarding and other elite schools which have sprawling grounds. Children are given relatively free reign during their downtime.

I was lucky enough to spend some time at a primary school outside London at which it took more than an hour to walk the perimeter of the grounds and woods, which we were allowed to roam freely. During our hour-and-a-half lunch breaks, we would play ‘manhunt’ in groups of 30. One boy would start off as the ‘hunter’ and would have to find and tackle another boy, who would then join the hunters, and go find and tackle others, and so on. A full game took hours, with a thrilling chase for the last man. Looking back, this feels like a relic of a lost age.

The decline in family formation is commonly met with proposals for subsidies and tax credits. But this is scooping water from a boat with a hole in the bottom.

Serious families have three options:

1. Buy their way out of a dislocated, illegible, and collapsing society;
2. Radicalize and embrace the *‘great sort’* (which sounds ‘based’ in theory but isn’t always fun in practice: communities which define themselves by their religious radicalism can devolve into purity spiraling, etc.);
3. Go through the exhausting effort of recreating every societal function inside the security of their private dwellings.

What fun pathways for prospective parents!

Our dear leaders gave made various insane policy decisions which makes the crushing cost of having children systematically entrenched, and - preposterously - in some circumstances a welcome feature. It’s now one of the only mechanisms left that allows functional people to live together.

Communities which solve this will see an explosion in children.
