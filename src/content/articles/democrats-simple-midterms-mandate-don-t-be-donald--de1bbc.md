# Democrats’ Simple Midterms Mandate: Don’t Be Donald Trump

*If they control Congress, their takeaway should be less sweeping than it probably will be.*

I do not have any better an idea of how Tuesday’s elections will turn out than you do. There are some things—markets, the movement of bird flocks, evolution—that are not predictable, neither as a practical matter nor, more important, *even in principle*. You never have the same traffic jam twice. Elections are like the weather: There are trends in which one can have some confidence, but the closer one moves to specificity the more unpredictable things get. It is a safe bet that the average day in Houston in August of this year will be hotter than the average day in January (and the average day in hell), but if you were wagering on the temperature spread between January 15 and August 9 at 4 p.m. on Runway 13R/31L at Hobby Airport to within 1 degree, then I’ll bet against you no matter what, because you aren’t going to get it right.

So, is James Talarico going to win in Texas? Who has the heat? Ask my wife (who sometimes will casually drop the word *psychrometric* in conversation) what the temperature in the living room is, and she’ll ask: *Where in the living room?* In the same way, yes, it seems that Talarico is running a little warmer than his Republican opponent in the race, the execrable and embarrassing Ken Paxton, but one must consider the margin of error in the thermometers. (Is the baby running a fever? The margin of error in common drugstore thermometers under real-world conditions is as high as ±3.7 degrees Fahrenheit, so ... maybe, maybe not. When the question comes up some morning at 2:43 a.m., this will seem to me more important than polling error margins.) Anybody who tells you that they know for sure how any competitive race is going to turn out is lying to you in the service of some financial, political, or personal interest.

If I were betting my own money on it, I would bet on a night that is bad for Republicans but not as bad for them as I’d like for it to be, with the result being a relatively modest but far from dispositive Democratic victory. What that probably looks like is control of the House and, if the Democrats perform on the high end of reasonable expectations, a very small margin in the Senate, though I would not be very surprised to see the upper chamber go either way. (And who knows what monkey wrenches miscreants such as John Fetterman may throw into the works post-election?) Democrats may pick up some unexpected wins in places that did not seem likely and may lose a few that they weren’t expecting to. I would not bet my own money on Gina Hinojosa winning the governorship in Texas, but I would not be very surprised if she did. Democrats may have a better night than expected—or Republicans may.

One thing that is a safe bet, I think: No matter how tiny, modest, limited, and underwhelming the Democrats’ success may be on Tuesday, any outcome that sees them taking control of the House will produce a great deal of idiotic braying about the Democrats’ new “mandate for change.” Some of that is just convention—American politicians just talk that way, for some reason—but considerably worse and more worrisome is that some of these fools are going to believe that nonsense, just as Joe Biden apparently became convinced that he had early-1937 Franklin Roosevelt levels of political clout and a mandate to do whatever popped into the ample available space in his head or was whispered into his ear. That went about as well for Biden as one would have expected, and Democrats who decide to try to run wild with a “mandate” after Tuesday will be setting themselves up for the same kind of failure.

Democrats are not going to take advice from me, and there is no particular reason they should. I am a party of one and do not represent any powerful faction or desirable electoral demographic. But I will offer some advice nonetheless on the theory that it might be useful for them to hear even if they will not act on it.

If the Democrats do have a big night, they will have a mandate, but only the most obvious one: The Democrats will have a *mandate to not be Donald Trump*. That is it.

That’s the same mandate Biden had last time around. And that is enough—or should be.

I am not among those who believe that there is no meaningful difference between the parties, that six of one is half a dozen of the other, etc. That said, the two sides in the 1927 Pharus-Säle brawl, which saw Nazi brownshirts duking it out with Stalinist KPD goons, had a great deal more in common with one another than either did with, say, F. A. Hayek, at the time a postgraduate student observing German developments, with growing apprehension, from Vienna. Like the two great totalitarian movements of the 20th century, what our Democrats and Republicans have in common with each other in 2026 is their worst tendencies: Republicans are not the only ones who have rejected the legitimacy of elections they lost, who have a corruption problem, who are in thrall to a particularly stupid and vicious kind of populism made worse by social-media culture, who act without sufficient sense of responsibility, patriotism, duty, and citizenship. It is true that these problems have been significantly worse among Republicans for a decade now—Donald Trump is partly a cause of that and partly a result of it. But enlightened Democrats surely appreciate that their party is vulnerable to the same vices as the GOP.

We sometimes talk about the policy situation in Washington using terms such as “gridlock” and “logjam,” as though the reality were that there were two powerful factions, each with a positive and definite political program, neither with the clout to quite get it done in the face of energized opposition and special-interest maneuvering. But that is not really the case.

When Biden took over for Trump in 2021, he and his congressional allies kept much of the worst of Trump’s economic policy, from tariffs and protectionism and crony corporatism to maintaining enormous deficits and rejecting any effort at entitlement reform. The Democrats did not get tricked into following Trump’s economic playbook in the Biden years. Rather, the Democrats kept the tariffs and the corporatism and the deficits because these are the product of *bipartisan* consensus—idiotic, dangerous, and destructive bipartisan consensus, to be sure, but bipartisan consensus nonetheless. Republicans have had two periods of unified “trifecta” government under Donald Trump, from 2017 to 2019 and from 2025 to the present, during which they have shown themselves unable or unwilling to advance substantive reforms to *anything*—taxes, spending, immigration, trade, national security—through Congress, sinking instead into the bog of Trump’s ad-hocracy. The result: economic and political stagnation; deficits approaching COVID-era levels and unmatched by any non-COVID period in modern history as extraordinary deficits become ordinary deficits; ruptured alliances and utter incoherence in international relations and military affairs; a business environment increasingly warped by corporatist subsidies, protectionism, and favor-granting. Even the success in closing the southern border is a tenuous thing that can, and may, be undone easily by a succeeding president, so little having been effected in law.

Of course Democrats have a mandate to not be Trump, however the election turns out. In a sense, everybody has a mandate to not be Trump: Trump’s approval numbers are down there with hemorrhoids. But the mandate to not be Trump is not the mandate to be Bernie Sanders or Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez—figures who are, in important ways, more like Trump than unlike Trump. The proper response to right-wing populism, extremism, vendetta politics, hysteria, etc. is not more of the same from a new group of newly empowered jackasses, old policies applied to a new enemies’ list.

I know that I am not going to get my best-case Democratic Party scenario—the one in which Democratic leaders go back and read a few years’ worth of editorials in *The Economist* and say, “Okay, we can do that!” But there are better and worse ways for the Democrats to proceed with whatever newfound power they have come January. Because I have a very strong preference for *boring* government, let’s consider the case of taxes.

At the moment, the Democrats talk about taxes in almost exclusively moralistic—and punitive—terms. This is an exercise in pure magical thinking: “If we do the thing that makes us feel morally satisfied, then the math will take care of itself.” But the math never takes care of itself. The purpose of a tax policy is not to pursue fairness, morality, social justice, or anything like that: The purpose of tax policy is to raise sufficient revenue while minimizing the inevitable damage and distortion that all taxes impose on an economy. The discussion can be maddening, as I know, having been through it so many times. It goes like this:

Some nice progressive: “If only billionaires would pay their fair share, then we could have all the spending we want and balance the budget, too.”

Pointed-headed right-wing ideologue: “Nope. Here are the numbers.”

SNP: “But that isn’t how I want it to be!”

P-HR-WI: “Your subjective sense of fairness has no effect on the underlying economic reality.”

SNP: “Jeff Bezos paid you to say that.”

It does not matter how many times you repeat the relevant facts—that we actually had slightly *lower* taxes (revenue as share of GDP) during the supposedly high-tax Eisenhower years than we have recently, that high-income people already pay a wildly disproportionate share of federal income tax, that the low notional rate on dividends is offset by the fact that these are paid out of business profits that already have been taxed at the much higher corporate rate, etc.—and people will cling to their superstitions and fictions and clichés as though their lives depended on it. Anything other than *think*.

Democrats could put forward a program that would reduce and stabilize the deficit while fortifying Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid. Or they could scratch that ol’ *Klassenkampf* itch. They could even do a bit of both, if they were creative: It wouldn’t be the worst idea in the world to: 1. get rid of the corporate income tax entirely but 2. tax capital gains at the ordinary income rate while 3. exempting capital gains in ordinary retirement accounts from taxation but 4. getting rid of the ceiling on the payroll tax and 5. sharply reducing Social Security and Medicare benefits for high-income beneficiaries. That isn’t *my* ideal program, but it isn’t my party, either. Get rid of a bunch of tariffs and trade barriers to help bring prices down and stabilize supply chains, make it easier to build a bunch of nuclear power plants (which will reduce greenhouse gas emissions and could power a whole bunch of desalination infrastructure in California and Texas), and take a progressive “abundance” approach to reforming construction permitting—that’s a good 10 years’ work that a great many libertarians and conservatives would not object to *very* much (yes, Grover Norquist et al. will howl about the payroll tax), that would contribute mightily to real prosperity, and that would look awfully good in comparison to what Republicans have been offering or are likely to offer.

In reality, any kind of Democratic policy agenda is likely to take a backseat to other Democratic priorities—especially the priority of adversarial oversight and investigation of the Trump administration, up to and including the possibility of a third impeachment of the president and the impeachment of underlings from J.D. Vance to Marco Rubio to Pete Hegseth. This is reasonable and appropriate, particularly given that even an intelligent and enlightened Democratic policy agenda—*especially* an intelligent and enlightened Democratic policy agenda, if such a thing should be exnihilated into being—would be difficult if not impossible to pass while the Democrats have only small margins in one or both chambers and while Trump remains president. (Should the Democrats take the House but not the Senate, then Republicans will still effectively control the initiative where policymaking is concerned but will continue to rely on unstable executive unilateralism.) The Democrats will face a complex combination of political crosswinds when dealing with that: from the Republicans, who will howl about double standards and opportunism and somehow expect to be taken seriously; from Democrats, who always risk falling prey to ersatz cleverness and will seek to game hearings and any possible impeachment for maximum political advantage, which isn’t inherently illegitimate but which generally leads to bad decision-making; from the general public, which may be tired of that kind of spectacle and which may feel that grandstanding in hearings doesn’t do much to address their affordability concerns, which are fundamental; and from business elites, whose most influential figures (many of whom have been practically supine in their efforts to court the goodwill of this White House) desire a stable political environment and who do not wish to be pulled between Democratic demands and a Republican administration that, for all of its intellectual and moral impotence, still controls the DOJ, the FBI, the Commerce Department, the Treasury, etc., and consequently has the power to make life hell for any given business or industry.

If the Democrats could channel their moralism and natural vindictiveness into oversight and investigation while keeping cool heads where policy-making is concerned, that would be just about ideal—or at least the best of all the plausible scenarios. But whatever the outcome of the election, Democrats should keep foremost in mind that whatever mandate they have is not a mandate to be mirror images of Trump and his party, running hysterically in a dozen different directions at once, ragefully lurching from one vendetta to the next and from one act of petty revenge-taking to another, attempting to govern as though the opposition did not exist and as though there were no tomorrow.

Because tomorrow is coming, and so is the next Election Day.

## And Furthermore ...

Last week, I wrote about the Trump faction’s last attempted *coup d’état* and the one they might be expected to try if the midterm elections go very badly for them. I pointed out in the piece that most of the major coups in the history of the Western world for the past couple of centuries—from the ones that brought Augusto Pinochet and Francisco Franco to power to the one that helped to clear the way for Adolf Hitler—is that they generally relied upon some legal pretext, sometimes before the fact, sometimes after, sometimes both. The fact that an interested party can make some kind of legal or constitutional argument for a coup does not make the coup any less of a coup.

Some of the response was the predictable insipid stuff: How dare you compare Trump to this or that murderous dictator! We aren’t building concentration camps and disappearing political dissidents or torturing them in sports stadiums! And that is true. But my argument is not about whether Trump is conducting a campaign of mass murder against political dissidents at home. He isn’t. He is conducting a campaign of mass murder of supposed drug smugglers at sea, but set that aside for the moment. My complaint is the attempted coup. As Jonah Goldberg sometimes observes, “Trump isn’t Hitler!” isn’t much of a defense, inasmuch as one can be pretty bad without being Hitler.

But here’s something to think about: Hitler wasn’t Hitler, either, until he was. By that I mean that most historians and biographers agree that when he came to power in 1933, Adolf Hitler did not have in mind extermination camps and related measures targeting the Jews of Europe and other hated groups–the program of genocide that has become, for good reason, synonymous with his name. Hitler was a vicious and violence-minded antisemite, without a doubt, but what came to be known as the Final Solution seems to have been an idea developed years after Hitler came to power and partly under the influence of other men. There were political concentration camps pretty quickly, but the first extermination camp did not start operating until 1941, after Hitler had been in power for nearly a decade.

Consider that category of “Bad, but not Hitler Bad” tyrants. Pinochet did not take power because he wanted to torture people and murder his political opponents; he tortured people and murdered his political opponents in order to consolidate power and keep it. The Spanish Nationalists didn’t want power because they wanted to murder Federico García Lorca—they murdered Federico García Lorca because they wanted power. Some of the goals of Franco and Pinochet were (and are) shared by people of goodwill who reject these monsters and their techniques—anticommunism is preeminent among these. (Anticommunism has a way of blinding Americans and some European liberals to some truly horrifying things. Hayek’s tolerant attitude toward Pinochet drew a gentle rebuke from no less a figure than Margaret Thatcher. (“I am sure you will agree that ... some of the measures adopted in Chile are quite unacceptable.”) Means and ends both matter.

I do not know whether Donald Trump and those around him would set up a Lubyanka or a Perm-36 if they had the chance to do so. I do not need to know. I know that Donald Trump and his gang attempted to overthrow the government of the United States by nullifying the 2020 election and illegally maintaining Trump in power. That is all I need to know. And if you are a politician with an “R” next to your name who cannot speak plainly and honestly about this—and there are damned few of those—then I know all I need to know about you, too.

## Elsewhere

Do read “This Data Center Crap” from Amelia Christmas Gramling in *The Dispatch*. A very tasty slice of American life. Excerpt:

> I was early—the courtroom hadn’t opened yet. So I stood outside with a white-mustachioed retiree named Jack Takach who asked me if I’d brought any snacks.
>
> Jack used to farm tobacco with my dad at a homestead called the Flying Frog Farm about 10 miles east of Franklin. He’d been at another court proceeding regarding the same data center in June that was a disappointment, or so I gathered, because it had been hard to hear and he had been hungry (Jack had stood up in the back of the courtroom as soon as the judge adjourned and bellowed “WHERE ARE THE SANDWICHES?”—but none materialized). Jack told me the data center was probably a foregone conclusion, nothing to be done: “Seems like money’s being spread around if you know what I mean.” He elaborated in a hushed voice that, from Jack, was still basically a scream. “Like the fix is in, you get me?”
>
> Still, he had come. The last time Jack had been adjacent to anything like a political action, he’d been a junior at Towson College protesting the Vietnam draft. Bobby Kennedy had just been assassinated. COINTELPRO was tapping phones. He spent the next 50 years in the woods in western Kentucky, where he built a geodesic dome and then a saltwater pool, and where he had done his best to build a home into which international politics could not penetrate. His presence at the courthouse meant the prospect of the data center had grown, even in Jack’s protracted hermitage, too real to ignore, and too close to avoid. It meant, too—despite what he told me—that he was still hopeful it could be stopped.
>
> Jack’s hopes, and those of many others in Simpson County, were pinned on an antique mall. Bright’s Antique World adjoins the tract of land, out on Steele Road, on which TenKey plans to build. It is owned by a married couple who met as songwriters in Nashville: Linda and Riley Bright.
>
> The Brights’ lawsuit was straightforward and highly localized: It was about two adjacent properties, two would-be neighbors. The Brights’ complaint was that the development TenKey had proposed—a hyperscale data center and on-site natural gas power plant—would be bad for business, i.e., that the smells, sounds, and general “disturbances” emitted by the plant would dissuade patrons from attending their outdoor markets. Simple enough. But the stakes of the case, as far as the rest of the town was concerned, were no less than existential. Denita Brady, a Franklin native who I met outside of the courthouse bathroom, told me she was afraid. “Used to be that in a world that was literally burning, Franklin was where we were still loving our neighbors, raising our kids, you know *still* the same town,” she said. “That’s how it was, anyway, before all this data center crap went down.”

## In Closing

I have four adorable little boys. People ask about the triplets often, because triplets are just kind of inherently interesting. They are three very distinct little men, but, every now and then, they do one of those spooky triplet things, where they all seem to be able to read each other’s minds. They don’t really know what Halloween is, but when I asked them what they were going to be, they all answered—as one—“deer.” I have no idea why. We do have many deer on our property most days. Their big brother decided to be a dragon. I asked him whether he intended to be a cute dragon or a scary dragon. “SCARY!” he insisted, clawing at the air and snarling.
