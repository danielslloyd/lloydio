# Politics Without Winners: Can Either Party Build a Majority Coalition?

## Executive Summary

In the American political system, the parties' purpose is to form enduring national coalitions. Look at almost any point in American history, and you will find a majority party working to sustain a complex coalition and a minority party hoping to recapture the majority. Today, however, American politics features two minority parties, and neither seems interested in building a national coalition. Close elections and narrow majorities dominate electoral politics more than at any other point in American history. This report explores the evolution of the party coalitions, considers their contemporary strengths and weaknesses using fresh data from AEI's Survey Center on American Life, and assesses which issues Democrats and Republicans can use to build a durable majority.

After the American constitutional system began, political parties materialized nearly immediately. Yet not until the chaos of the 1824 presidential election were the parties truly formalized and institutionalized. Martin Van Buren and others argued for developing two broad, durable parties to subsume personal ambition and moderate divisions. This party system formed around the intense battles of 19th-century politics and gradually fell into a pattern of shifting majority coalitions.

The realigning election of 1896, which expanded the Republican coalition among diverse groups, including urban workers, began a period of Republican dominance that lasted until the Great Depression. Between 1896 and 1913, the economy more than doubled in size, and real per capita income rose by 2.5 percent per year.

In the wake of the 1929 stock market crash, a new governing coalition emerged under Franklin D. Roosevelt's leadership. The New Deal coalition owed its electoral success to the perception that Democrats were the party of the people—unionized workers, cross-class white southerners, and ethnic minorities who united against a Republican Party that they perceived as too friendly to business and the wealthy. Even as Dwight Eisenhower captured the presidency, this coalition returned massive Democratic majorities to the House and Senate.

Not until the late 1960s did the New Deal coalition fracture. Democratic leaders embraced a bevy of new social movements, especially the civil rights movement, and white working-class voters began to abandon the party. Combined with the widely perceived failure of Democratic economic management in the 1970s, this led to a new era of Republican dominance.

After resurgences during the Clinton and Obama years, Democrats once again found themselves on a rocky road in 2016. By a massive margin, Donald Trump won support from white working-class voters—including many who had voted for Barack Obama four years prior. In 2020, though Joe Biden narrowly defeated Trump, Democrats up and down the ballot started to slip with black and Hispanic voters, particularly those without a college degree.

Today, the Republican coalition relies heavily on white working-class voters and is increasingly weak among white college-educated voters. The GOP generally does poorly among non-white voters, especially blacks, but is starting to do better among non-white working-class voters. Geographically, Republicans dominate rural and small-town America and perform much better in outer suburbs and exurbs than in more educated, affluent inner suburbs. They get walloped in dense urban areas.

The Democratic coalition, on the other hand, has become increasingly dependent on the votes of college graduates, particularly white college graduates. Its long-standing and worsening weakness is among white working-class voters, still a vast constituency despite their steadily declining weight in the electorate. The burgeoning non-white population continues to vote heavily Democratic, but the Democratic margins here are starting to decline, especially among Hispanic and working-class voters.

Geographically, the Democrats' support base is highly polarized. They dominate urban areas and run up ever-bigger margins in inner suburbs, but their strength diminishes away from the urban core. And in much of rural and small-town America, their brand is simply toxic.

What the Republican and Democratic coalitions have in common is enough strength to stalemate the other party but not enough to dominate. As a result, a noxious back-and-forth has defined American politics for a generation. Can the deadlock be broken?

Our analysis, based on a proprietary survey and publicly available data, reveals which issues each party can use to create a majority coalition. Republicans could capitalize on Democratic cultural radicalism—including on immigration, crime, and identity politics—energy realism, and patriotism. Democrats, meanwhile, hold clear advantages with their positions on abortion, health care, and adherence to political norms. But the key issues of economic prosperity and America's place in the world lack a defining advantage for either party.

Stalemate is not the American party system's natural equilibrium. Both parties have avenues to build a durable majority, but they must first recognize where they have gone wrong. Only then can each consciously build a dominant coalition.

## Introduction

The American party system is in an era of dysfunction. That dysfunction takes various forms, but at its core is a failure to build broad, national coalitions, which is the fundamental work of America's two major parties.

If you had looked in on American politics at almost any point in our history, you would have found a majority party working to sustain a complex coalition and a minority party defining itself against the majority and working to grow and overcome it. But for a generation now, American politics has featured two minority parties, each defining itself primarily against the other and neither capable of sustaining a durable majority. Elections have been close, congressional majorities have been narrow, control of Congress and the presidency has shifted back and forth, and Americans have sensed they are living in a persistent crisis of legitimacy.

If history is any guide, this phase will pass, and one dominated by a relatively stable majority coalition will replace it. But that will not happen on its own. Either party may have a path to becoming that next majority, but for either to break through, it must understand its failures. This, in turn, would require an awareness of the American party system's historical purpose and character, the party coalitions' modern evolution, the electorate's changing demographics and priorities, and what building a durable majority now would take.

This report offers an overview of what each party must consider to pursue a durable majority. It begins by explaining the American party system's basic character and aims. Both parties seem to have forgotten their purpose, which in America's system (unlike those of some other democracies) is to form a durable, national majority coalition. The report then provides a brief history of the party coalitions' evolution, especially since the New Deal. Next, it considers contemporary party coalitions, drawing on substantial public opinion data, including recent survey work by AEI's Survey Center on American Life that is analyzed in detail here for the first time. Finally, it concludes with some observations on the prospects for the next phase of the American party system, drawing on the history and data reviewed here.

## I. Competing National Coalitions

The American party system is distinct from those of most other democracies because, with a few brief exceptions, it has involved two broad, national parties. Rather than a larger number of parties representing narrow or focused swaths of the electorate that form governing coalitions with other parties after winning elected office, America has two leading parties representing vast swaths of society that must form broad, internal coalitions to succeed and must then attempt to win public majorities.

This duopoly of coalitional parties was only partially intentional, as it grew around a system that was originally intended to avoid durable parties altogether. Party politics took shape under a heavy shadow of suspicion in the early American republic. Although they had a highly sophisticated grasp of political culture, institutional design, and social psychology, the framers of the American constitution operated in a moment—in the wake of the American Revolution and amid an upheaval in Britain's party coalitions—when it seemed possible for a democratic polity to function without formal, organized parties. The leading framers were intensely wary of parties because they identified them fundamentally with factions, which represented the interests of a part of American society against the whole.

In advancing the case for the Constitution, some of its foremost champions argued that its institutions would not require permanent parties. "We are attempting by this constitution to abolish factions," Alexander Hamilton told the New York ratifying convention, "and to unite all parties for the general welfare." Upon completing his second term as president, George Washington devoted a key portion of his Farewell Address to warning of the dangers of partisanship, arguing that "the common and continual mischiefs of the spirit of Party are sufficient to make it the interest and the duty of a wise People to discourage and restrain it."

According to this conception, parties draw people away from the interests of the whole society toward the particular or special interests of part of society. But formal political parties ultimately exist not to embody divisions but to organize coalitions. A democratic republic's politics require such organization, which the constitutional institutions cannot facilitate on their own. Parties, therefore, proved unavoidable almost immediately after the Constitution launched the new system.

Some of the most heated debates in the First and Second Congresses involved regional economic tensions and the separation of powers: Hamilton, while serving as Washington's Treasury secretary, proposed bold measures to facilitate commerce and industry in the new republic's economy and found himself intensely opposed by Thomas Jefferson and James Madison. To organize the opposition, Jefferson and Madison began to assemble a party apparatus that came to be known as the Republicans or (mostly in retrospect) the Democratic-Republican Party. The forces that the Democratic-Republicans opposed then coalesced, in response, into the Federalist Party.

These two early parties offered preliminary examples of the system America's constitutional architecture would always tend to produce: two broad parties, each striving for a national coalition, with regional, demographic, economic, and ideological divisions.

Jefferson's Democratic-Republicans had their core regional base in the South and an ideology protective of agrarian priorities against commercial and industrial northern interests. They favored decentralization and strong state governments, individual liberty, and free trade. And they tended to side with France over Britain in European affairs.

The Federalists' power base was in New England, and their vision of America's future was industrial and commercial. They favored a strong national government, backed domestic manufacturing (and therefore some degree of protectionism), wanted a robust banking system, sided with Britain over France in European affairs, and wanted public policy to promote economic growth, modernization, and infrastructure. But both parties sought (and found) support in all the new nation's regions and through various crisscrossing appeals.

One way to understand these two coalitions is that they had two sets of interests, and each sought its own advantage. But another is that they had two visions for the future of America as a whole, and each sought to advance what it thought was best for the nation, not just for its part of the nation. Since there were just two major parties, victory for either would require winning the support of an electoral majority. That meant both had to argue for the good of the nation, not just of themselves, and appeal to uncommitted voters. Each, in other words, had to construct a broad and complicated coalition.

Thus, the American party system offered a way to avoid the framers' worst fears about factionalism; two parties competing for a majority must at least try to rise above narrow interests. This dynamic set the pattern that has dominated the system ever since.

But American politics has almost always involved two broad coalitional parties of this sort, not only because of the earliest national political debates but also for reasons that run deep into the structure of America's government—above all, the presidential selection process. The Electoral College never quite worked as intended, and the 12th Amendment reformed it fairly quickly, in a way that reinforced the partisan duopoly.

As originally conceived in the Constitution, each presidential elector had two votes in the Electoral College and thus voted for two candidates. A candidate who received the votes of an absolute majority of all the electors nationwide would be elected president, and the candidate with the second-highest number of votes would become vice president. If two candidates tied or no one won support from a majority of the electors, then the House of Representatives would choose the president, and again, the second-highest vote-getter would become vice president.

But as the consensus of the founding era dissipated and broad ideological divisions began to emerge, it quickly became apparent that this system could result in two strange and undesirable outcomes. First, the individuals elected president and vice president were the election's two leading candidates, so they might have run against each other. They could be political opponents and thus not well suited to cooperating. Second, even if they were not opponents and had run together or attracted the same electors, each might receive the same number of votes (since each elector could cast two), creating a tie that the House of Representatives must resolve.

These two problems were not just arcane or theoretical possibilities: As soon as Washington departed the scene, they both materialized immediately. The 1796 election produced a president and vice president (John Adams and Jefferson) who were opponents unsuited to working together, and the 1800 election resulted in a tie (between Jefferson and Aaron Burr) that the House of Representatives had to resolve.

To address these problems, the 12th Amendment was ratified in 1804. It changed the system so each member of the Electoral College would cast just one vote for president, not two, and a separate, single vote for vice president. The winner of a majority of electoral votes in each case would win the office. If no one won a majority, then the House would decide the presidential election, and the Senate would decide the vice presidential election.

This meant presidential and vice presidential candidates tended to run on a joint ticket and work together when elected. But because each elector now had only one presidential vote, the Electoral College turned into a simple rubber stamp on each state's popular vote. Electors were chosen based on their commitment to vote for a particular candidate, so the mass of voters was essentially voting for the president, and the states' popular elections determined the outcome.

This altered system significantly increased the need for some coordination and organization before presidential contests. The Electoral College would not deliberatively sift or select candidates, and if more than two serious candidates ran, the House would routinely be called on to decide presidential elections. Some winnowing would have to precede the election. Essential to this process were organized parties—ideally, just two large ones that each worked to build a national majority coalition, since winning required an absolute Electoral College majority.

In addition, many (though not all) American legislative elections soon used plurality (first-past-the-post) voting. In this system, one seat is at stake in each election, which the top vote-getter wins. (In contrast, some other democracies divide multiple seats among several parties in proportion to their share of the vote.) This approach encourages parties to seek absolute majorities in congressional as well as state and local elections, which created an additional strong incentive for forming just two broad parties throughout the system.

For a time, the ad hoc party system that emerged around the controversies of the Washington administration seemed capable of meeting this need. But Jefferson's Democratic-Republicans soon became so dominant that the party system all but ceased to function. The Federalists effectively disappeared by the time James Monroe was elected to succeed Madison in 1816, becoming the third two-term Democratic-Republican president in a row. That stretch came to be known as the Era of Good Feelings, but by its end, with the party system vitiated and key regional and ideological divisions contained in one party rather than distributed between two, the risks inherent in the transformed Electoral College rules emerged with a vengeance.

The 1824 election featured four major candidates—Andrew Jackson, John Quincy Adams, Henry Clay, and William Crawford—all of whom were Democratic-Republicans. They divided the vote, and none secured a majority in the Electoral College. This sent the election to the House, where Adams (who had come in second to Jackson in both the popular and electoral votes) won the presidency.

Without functional parties, the presidential selection process fell into chaos. Many observers worried that every subsequent election would devolve into a multicandidate contest of personalities, with factions gathering around high-profile figures rather than governing visions and the House choosing every president. These concerns highlighted a distinct inadequacy in the constitutional framework.

A few key Democrats, notably a US senator from New York named Martin Van Buren, grasped that the problem required a revitalized two-party system. In a posthumously published memoir, Van Buren explained how he understood the danger that presented itself in the 1824 election:

In the place of two great parties arrayed against each other in a fair and open contest for the establishment of principles in the administration of Government which they respectively believed most conducive to the public interest, the country was overrun with personal factions. These having few higher motives for the selection of their candidates or stronger incentives to action than individual preferences or antipathies, moved the bitter waters of political agitation to their lowest depths.

The framers of the Constitution had feared such personalism, which pointed toward the demagoguery that had historically undone many republics, and they hoped the Electoral College would avert it. Yet without "two great parties arrayed against each other," it seemed the constitutional system alone could not do so.

Therefore, Van Buren and others argued for developing two broad, durable, national parties, which would each seek to become a majority coalition to advance its chosen candidates for the presidency, the vice presidency, Congress, and state and local offices. These parties ought not to be regional, to avoid exacerbating geographic divisions. And they should not be factional, representing narrow interests or (as we would say now) single-issue platforms and aiming to join governing coalitions upon being elected. Rather, each party should adopt a general governing approach and agenda and appeal to persuadable, uncommitted voters.

Implicit in this vision was the idea that a democratic society's politics would fall naturally into two broad camps—that left and right, in today's vernacular, had some genuine significance as ways of thinking about the great questions of the day. But although each broad vision might speak forcefully to intellectually or ideologically inclined voters, numerous voters might swing between them based on their sense of the country's immediate needs and the appeal of each party's policies and candidates.

Van Buren argued that this core division would not be imposed on American society; rather, it was already present. The parties merely institutionalized it in a particular way. In the wake of the 1824 election, they would have to think more carefully and explicitly about just what that way ought to be and use party committees, conventions, and other mechanisms to sift and select candidates and facilitate electioneering and governing.

Van Buren's party system was a kind of missing piece in the constitutional puzzle. Its logic was distinctly Madisonian: The party would subsume personal ambition beneath institutional purpose, moderate divisions, use the selection process for an office as a means of forming people to perform that office well, and pave the way for accommodative governing in both elected branches. Party conventions and similar nominating processes would allow elites to manage the mass democracy's options, ensuring candidates were qualified and broadly appealing. And by controlling the number of presidential candidates, the system would avoid sending presidential elections to the House. Indeed, not a single election has gone to the House since 1824, thanks in no small part to the parties adopting the sorts of processes Van Buren and his allies proposed.

Van Buren's key insight was one that contemporary Americans still find difficult to wrap their heads around, though it remains as true as ever: Parties as institutions actually restrain partisanship and moderate its harms. Weaker parties or a politics without functional parties would mean more intense partisanship. Stronger parties tend to result in a more temperamentally moderate politics and in more bargaining and compromise. To achieve the framers' goal of restraining partisan zeal, Van Buren proposed a means they had repudiated: strengthening and formalizing two broad, institutionally assertive yet ideologically multifaceted national parties. By the mid-19th century, his vision of the American party system was a reality. And at the heart of that system was the imperative to build broad, national coalitions.

## II. Shifting Coalitions

The two parties of that age first emerged from the Jeffersonian coalition during the Era of Good Feelings. One of them, which formed around Jackson, was populist—committed to resisting the power of elites, bankers, and industrialists to advance the common American's interests. This party's members called themselves Democrats. The other party, which formed around John Quincy Adams and Clay, feared Jackson would usher in an age of demagogues and advanced instead an agenda of modernization and national greatness that evinced far greater trust in elites, industrialization, and the emerging market economy. This party's members—seeking to identify with the founding generation without adopting the discredited name of the Federalists—came to call themselves Whigs.

In modern parlance, the Whigs were broadly conservative. Their coalition involved professionals and business owners, evangelical Christians, and large parts of the early 19th century's growing middle class. They resisted presidential power and populism and favored a national bank and federal spending on infrastructure.

The Democrats, whose coalition tended to be larger and dominant in this period, were supported especially by farmers and the emerging industrial workforce. They were that age's populist party, calling for purer majority rule, opposing the power of banks and the use of paper money, and resisting federal encroachment on the states' prerogatives.

Both parties had national reach, though the Whigs were strongest in New England and the Democrats in the South. Partly as a result, the Democrats generally defended slavery and even supported expanding it into new territories, while the Whigs were divided on the issue but included many firm abolitionists.

In the 1850s, slavery's geographic expansion dominated American politics. The Democratic Party supported such expansion (favoring the repeal of the Missouri Compromise and the enactment of the Kansas-Nebraska Act), but the Whig Party's internal divisions persisted and ultimately became unsustainable. Northern Whigs increasingly focused their opposition to the Democrats on the slavery question, gradually organizing as a new Republican Party. Southern Whigs focused instead on opposition to immigration and on anti-Catholicism, becoming the Know-Nothing movement and the American Party.

By 1856, the Whigs had effectively ceased to exist, and that year's presidential election featured a three-way race among Democrats, Republicans, and Know-Nothings. By 1860, the Democrats had split along regional lines, and the party system collapsed as the nation fell into civil war.

In the war's wake, the two-party system soon reassembled. The Republicans represented northern economic and business interests and the broad middle class, and the Democrats represented not only the resentful South but increasingly also urban immigrants, Western farmers, and northern industrial workers who felt locked out of the new elite. As industrialization intensified economic inequalities in the decades that followed, Democrats returned to their populist roots, while Republicans took up the mantle of economic growth, mobility, and moral and religious appeals.

Ideas from the Progressive movement eventually infused both parties, and the final decades of the 19th century—what has become known to us as the Gilded Age—saw an unusual, extended period of parity between the parties. From the mid-1870s to the mid-1890s, a time political scientist Morris Fiorina has called "the Era of No Decision," congressional majorities were highly unstable, and control of the White House shifted back and forth. The winner of the Electoral College vote lost the popular vote twice, in 1876 and 1888, and exceedingly close elections created recurring crises of legitimacy. This was the closest analog in American history to the present extended period of unstable majorities.

That age of instability ended near the close of the 19th century with the realigning election of 1896. This election's precursor and a symptom of the period's instability was the farmers' revolt. Through the Grange movement, the Farmers' Alliance, and finally the People's (Populist) Party, organized farmers pressed an ambitious reform agenda focused on limiting the power of big banks, brokers, and merchants and making the government more democratic. The Omaha Platform, which the People's Party adopted at its 1892 convention, called for various reforms, including the abolition of national banks; a graduated income tax; direct election of senators; civil service reform; an eight-hour workday; government control of all railroads, telegraphs, and telephones; and the replacement of the gold standard with free silver coinage. The last provision was designed to combat the deflation of farm prices and help farmers pay their debts.

In the 1892 election, the People's Party fared extremely well for a third party in America, pulling over a million votes—8 percent of the total—and carrying five states (Colorado, Idaho, Kansas, Nevada, and North Dakota). This strong showing raised the political profile of serious reform. But the party would not last long, since the Democrats embraced free silver (though little else on the farmers' agenda) under William Jennings Bryan in 1896. This led the People's Party to make Bryan its presidential candidate as well and essentially ended the party's role as an independent political force.

The result was a disaster for the Democrats and Populists. Both parties were indelibly identified with the economic interests of rural farmers, which, in this case, directly conflicted with those of urban workers. Free silver would certainly have helped farmers, but urban workers would wind up with paychecks that bought less, thereby lowering their standard of living. Moreover, William McKinley and the Republicans defended high protective tariffs, while the Democrats and Populists opposed them. Again, this counterposed the interests of urban workers, who benefited from protection of their industries, to those of rural farmers.

The 1896 election, despite Bryan's efforts to invoke a coalition of the "toiling masses" across the cities and countryside, pitted the interests of a declining farmer class against those of a rising urban worker class. Moreover, Bryan's impassioned pleas were to voters who had experienced 15 years of stagnant living standards. This meant that, despite their discontent, voters were too fearful and unsettled to support his agenda.

The Democrats lost miserably, driven by uniformly sharp losses in urban areas like Baltimore, Maryland (19 percentage points); Boston, Massachusetts (19 points); New York (17 points); and Philadelphia, Pennsylvania (16 points). While some of the urban working-class vote share would return to the Democrats in future presidential elections—and remain there for many state and local elections—the Republicans would persistently claim a significant share of this vote.

A key effect of the realigning election of 1896 was—paradoxically, since the common contemporary interpretation was that conservative Republicans had overcome radical Democrats—to spread reform sentiment more uniformly across the two parties. Mugwumps, or ex-Republican urban reformers who had joined the Democrats in the 1880s, returned to their original party.

Significantly, McKinley's election ushered in an era of strong economic growth. Poor harvests in Europe increased the demand for American farm products, while gold suddenly became more available (thanks to Alaska's gold rush, among other factors), loosening credit and the money supply. That eliminated the deflation that had particularly bedeviled farmers, while the rise of mass production in sectors like auto and steel boosted American industry. As a result, the intense conflict between free silver and tariff protection swiftly faded into irrelevance.

Between 1896 and 1913, the economy more than doubled, and real per capita income rose by 2.5 percent per year. Unemployment, which was still at 14 percent in 1896, fell to 4 percent by 1901 and stayed near that level until World War I, when it fell even lower. Manufacturing workers fared particularly well, with their annual earnings rising steadily and spurting from $550 to $900 between 1908 and 1917.

Buoyed by these changes, Republicans rode their expanded coalition into dominance. They won the White House in seven of the nine presidential elections from 1896 through 1928, dominating the North and West and edging out the Democrats in the Midwest by emphasizing core economic issues, integrity in government, and moral reform. It would take a dramatic economic disaster to break their majority and produce another realigning election in 1932.

The precursor to this realigning election was the economically rocky 1920s. America suffered four recessions between 1918 and 1927, with a particularly sharp one in 1920–21. (A fifth, which initiated the Great Depression, would come in 1929.) By 1921, price deflation had returned, affecting both industrial and farm prices. Indeed, economic conditions in the early postwar years largely resembled those of the difficult 1880s and early 1890s.

However, the economy recovered, posting particularly strong growth in 1922–23, 1926, and 1929, right before the stock market crash. It is to these years, which featured a surging stock market and the rise of the consumer economy and mass entertainment, that we owe our image of the roaring twenties as an era of unmatched prosperity. Actually, overall economic progress in 1918–29 was considerably slower than in the Progressive Era (1.8 percent vs. 2.5 percent growth in annual per capita income), and much of it was unevenly distributed.

Democrats, hoping to capitalize on urban workers' and rural farmers' emerging discontent, made the reformist Al Smith their presidential nominee in 1928. Smith, an Irish Catholic born and bred in New York City, was perhaps not perfectly suited to appeal to rural America, but he nevertheless made significant Democratic gains there. His loss against conservative Republican Herbert Hoover, who carried 58 percent of the popular vote and 444 electoral votes, in some important respects prefigured the far different electoral results of 1932.

## III. The New Deal Coalition

The campaign of 1932 occurred against the backdrop of the Great Depression, with unemployment at 24 percent. For their standard-bearer, the Democrats turned to Franklin D. Roosevelt, a longtime progressive in the party who was governor of New York and had run for vice president on the 1920 Democratic ticket. FDR ran as a full-throated progressive who promised to use the tools of government to build from the bottom up and help the "forgotten man." For this emphasis, many claimed he was inciting a class war, including his rival for the nomination, 1928 Democratic candidate Smith. For Smith and others who considered themselves progressive, FDR went too far and verged on anti-business radicalism.

But voters saw it differently. FDR was elected in a landslide, with 57 percent of the vote to Hoover's 40 percent, and carried every state save Connecticut, Delaware, Maine, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, and Vermont. This was the first appearance of the New Deal coalition, based on Democratic domination of the rising class of blue-collar workers (now two and a half times as numerous as America's farmers and farmworkers), especially urban Catholic ethnic minorities and workers in mass production industries. This coalition included blacks and Jews, who suffered from discrimination in addition to economic problems, and white southerners, who were historically attached to the Democratic Party.

With this coalition backing him and a dire situation confronting the country, FDR launched a wave of reforms that considerably expanded the government's role in regulating the economy. This was consistent with the aims and philosophy of early 20th-century Progressivism but surpassed anything Progressives had attempted.

In FDR's famous first 100 days in office, he got Congress to create the Civilian Conservation Corps to provide jobs, the Federal Emergency Relief Administration to support urban incomes, and the Agricultural Adjustment Administration to support farm prices and refinance mortgages. Substantial funds were appropriated for new job-creating public works programs, which eventually evolved into the Works Progress Administration. Congress passed the Emergency Banking Act, reopening banks and instituting a bank inspection regime. It also passed the Glass-Steagall Act—which strengthened bank safety, controlled speculation, and created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation—and the Federal Securities Act, which mandated full disclosure of financial information in stock sales.

In these first 100 days and thereafter, FDR was under strong pressure to push the envelope in a progressive direction. This pressure came from not just Congress, where progressive Democrats were in a mood for decisive action, but also outside Congress and, in some cases, outside the major party structure. Father Charles Coughlin of Michigan had a radio audience of 10 million and sought to organize a National Union for Social Justice. Francis Townsend of California championed a radical pension plan, forming Townsend clubs across the country. Also in California, Upton Sinclair ran for governor in 1934 on his End Poverty in California program.

Then there were Huey Long in Louisiana and his Share Our Wealth movement, Floyd B. Olson of the Minnesota Farmer-Labor Party, and the followers of Robert M. La Follette in Wisconsin, who left the GOP in 1934 and formed a state-level Progressive Party that swept the state. Perhaps most important, there were the labor unions, especially the insurgent industrial unions that eventually formed the Congress of Industrial Organizations. Their unstinting support for continuing and deepening the New Deal was crucial to its reforms.

With this pressure and deep economic difficulties to overcome, progressivism advanced for much of the 1930s. The Securities and Exchange Commission was formed to strengthen stock market regulation. The Tennessee Valley Authority and Rural Electrification Administration were established. The US Housing Authority was created to provide housing for low-income families.

Congress passed the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, beginning modern drug regulation. The Social Security system was established. The Wagner Act and the establishment of the National Labor Relations Board encouraged union organizing. And Congress passed the Fair Labor Standards Act, which specified the 40-hour workweek, mandated time and a half for overtime work, prohibited most child labor, and set a minimum wage.

The economy remained dismal throughout this transformation of American capitalism: Per capita income did not recover to its pre-Depression level until 1940, and double-digit unemployment continued until America entered World War II. (Note, however, that 1934–36, when some of the most important New Deal legislation—the Wagner Act on collective bargaining, the Social Security Act, and the Rural Electrification Act—was passed, featured exceptionally fast economic growth, with over 10 percent gross domestic product growth per year.)

The electoral success of the Democrats' New Deal coalition depended, above all, on Democrats being perceived as the party of "the people" (a term widely used in the 1930s) and the Republicans as the party of business and the wealthy. This perception associated the Republicans with the stock market crash and Great Depression. It credited the Democrats with the economy's recovery and subsequent prosperity (regardless of whether, as some economists argue, World War II was the real cure for the Great Depression) and programs that empowered and delivered economic security to the working and middle classes. In his 1932 and 1936 campaigns, Roosevelt framed this perception politically and in populist terms—as a defense of the "forgotten man" and the "average citizen" against the "money changers" and the "economic royalists."

World War II itself was a time of stunning economic dynamism in the US: From 1941 to 1945, per capita income rose by over 9 percent annually. Of course, that wasn't sustainable. But even after the war, the United States enjoyed almost 30 years of rapid economic advance. Building on strong economic growth and rising incomes for the middle class and poor, Democrats continued and refined the New Deal's regulatory approach, social protections, and emphasis on economic security measures. And with Lyndon Johnson's Great Society reforms of the mid-1960s, the Democrats dramatically expanded government action in all these areas.

In this era, New Deal Democrats based their wordview on the Democratic Party's historical populist commitment to the average working American and their own experience battling the Great Depression (and building their political coalition) through increased government spending and regulation and the promotion of labor unions. Their philosophy was simple, even if its application was complex: Government should help the average person through public spending. Capitalism needs regulation to work properly. Labor unions are good. Putting money in the average person's pocket is more important than addressing rarefied worries about the quality of life. Traditional morality should be respected, not challenged. Racism should be opposed, but the party should not depart from its main mission of materially uplifting the average American.

This worldview, which had deep roots in an economy dominated by mass production industries, was politically focused on workers in those industries, who were overwhelmingly white. The Democrats' dominance among these voters was the key to their political success. To be sure, there were important divisions among these voters—by country of origin, religion (especially Protestantism vs. Catholicism), and region—that greatly complicated their politics, but the New Deal Democrats mastered these complications and maintained a deep base among them.

Of course, the New Deal coalition, as originally forged, included most blacks and was certainly cross-class, especially among Jews and southerners. But the prototypical coalition member was a white worker in a unionized factory. The party also included white blue-collar workers who weren't in unions or who toiled in other blue-collar settings, such as construction and transportation. These voters provided the numbers for Roosevelt's four election victories and Harry Truman's narrow victory in 1948. They also provided political support for the emerging welfare state, with its implicit social contract and greatly expanded role for the government.

Even in the 1950s, with Republican Dwight Eisenhower as president, the white working class continued to put Democrats in Congress and support the expansion of the welfare state and robust public investment. This era, from the late 1940s to the mid-1960s, created the world's first mass middle class—which even factory workers could enter, because they could earn relatively comfortable livings without high levels of education or professional skills. This was a middle class, in other words, that working-class whites could reasonably aspire to and frequently managed to join.

Democrats therefore depended on the white working class for political support, and the white working class depended on the Democrats to run the government and economy in a way that kept the upward escalator to the middle class moving. Social and cultural issues were not particularly important to this mutually beneficial relationship; indeed, they had only a peripheral role in the uncomplicated progressivism animating the Democratic Party of the 1930s, 1940s, and 1950s. But that arrangement, and that uncomplicated progressivism, could not survive the decline of mass production industries and the rise of postindustrial capitalism. By the mid-1960s, the New Deal coalition was fraying.

First, the white working class transformed. In 1948, about two-thirds of the workforce consisted of white men, the bulk of whom had blue-collar manufacturing and construction jobs or service jobs, such as janitorial or warehouse work. These men were heavily unionized, especially in certain areas of the country: By the late 1940s, unions claimed 60 percent or more of the northern blue-collar workforce. But that demographic profile did not last; as women entered the workforce in large numbers, unionization declined, and manufacturing declined as a share of working-class jobs and the larger economy.

Second, as the white working class changed, reducing the size and influence of the Democrats' traditional blue-collar constituencies, the evolution of postindustrial capitalism created new constituencies and movements with new demands. These constituencies wanted more from the welfare state than steady economic growth, copious infrastructure spending, and the opportunity to raise a family in the traditional manner.

During the 1960s, these new demands on the welfare state came to a head. Americans' concerns about their quality of life overflowed from wanting a two-car garage to wanting clean air and water and safe automobiles, from wanting higher wages to wanting government-guaranteed health care in old age, and from wanting equal job opportunities for men of different backgrounds to also wanting them for women and blacks. From these concerns came the environmental, consumer, civil rights, and feminist movements.

Of these movements, the one with the most far-reaching political effects was the civil rights movement, which demanded equality and economic progress for black America. Race divided the New Deal coalition, and the Democratic establishment—to its credit—ultimately sided with civil rights. This resulted in a great victory for the American ideal of equal opportunity but created huge political difficulties for the Democrats among their white working-class supporters.

But if race was the chief instrument that tore apart the New Deal coalition, it was by no means the only one. White working-class voters reacted poorly to the extremes with which the rest of the new social movements—such as feminism, the anti-war movement, environmentalism, and social libertarianism—became identified. The New Deal's uncomplicated commitments to government spending, economic regulation, and labor unions that had defined Democratic progressivism for more than 30 years suddenly provided the party with little guidance for dealing with an explosion of potential new constituencies. These constituencies' demands for equality and new understanding of the quality of life started to redefine progressivism, and the Democrats struggled to catch up.

Initially, Democratic politicians responded to these changes as politicians had done since time immemorial: They sought to co-opt these new movements by absorbing many of their demands while holding on to their party's basic ideology and governing style. That is, the Democrats didn't change their fundamental commitment to the New Deal welfare state; instead, they grafted on support for all the new constituencies' key demands. After Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act in 1964, the party gave the feminist, anti-war, consumer, and environmental movements prominent places in its coalition. This reflected politicians' standard interest in capturing the votes of new constituencies and the ongoing expansion of what it meant to be a Democrat, particularly a progressive one.

## IV. The Emerging Republican Majority

There was no guarantee, of course, that Democrats' gains among their new constituencies wouldn't be more than counterbalanced by losses in their old constituency—the white working class—which had precious little interest in expanding the definitions of "progressive" and "Democrat."

Indeed, in 1972, the nomination and disastrous defeat of George McGovern—an enthusiastic proponent of all the changes in the party—seemed to demonstrate this. McGovern's commitment to the traditional Democratic welfare state was unmistakable, but so was his commitment to the social movements and constituencies that were reshaping the party and whose demands were enshrined in his campaign platform. That made it easy for Richard Nixon's campaign to typecast McGovern as the candidate of "acid, amnesty, and abortion." The white working class accordingly supported Nixon overwhelmingly at the polls, casting 70 percent of its votes for the Republican candidate and contributing to his unprecedented 49-state Electoral College win.

Indeed, just how far the Democratic Party fell in the eyes of the white working class during that time can be seen by comparing the white working class's average vote for the Democrats in 1960–64 (55 percent) versus 1968–72 (35 percent). That's a drop of 20 points, from over half of these voters to just over one-third. The Democrats were the party of the white working class no longer.

With the sharp economic recession and political scandals of 1973–74, the Democrats developed enough political momentum to retake the White House in 1976, when Jimmy Carter narrowly defeated Gerald Ford. But the Democratic political revival did not last long. Not only did the Carter administration fail to defuse white working-class hostility to the new social movements, but also economic events—including the stagflation of the late 1970s—conspired to sharpen that hostility. Though stagflation (inflation and unemployment plus slow economic growth) first appeared during the 1973–75 recession, it grew during the Carter administration and peaked on the eve of the 1980 election. As the economy slid once more into recession, the inflation rate that year was 12.5 percent. Combined with an unemployment rate of 7.1 percent, it produced a misery index of nearly 20 percent.

Stagflation fed racial resentments—about high taxes for welfare (which presumably went primarily to minorities) and affirmative action. It also sowed doubts about the Democrats' ability to manage the economy and made Republican and business-oriented explanations of stagflation—blaming it on government regulation, high taxes, and spending—more plausible. In 1978, these arguments fueled a nationwide tax revolt. In 1980, these factors reproduced the massive 1968 and 1972 flights of white working-class voters from the Democratic tickets. The stage was set for the Reagan revolution.

Reagan's victory in 1980 was decisive and, in the Electoral College, certainly a landslide. He won 44 states and dominated every region of the country. Republicans won a Senate majority for the first time since 1952. Although the Democrats retained control of the House, 70 of the 78 southern Democrats would support Reagan's agenda, effectively giving him a majority on numerous key issues.

At the center of Reagan's new coalition were the same voters who had anchored every majority coalition for a century. In the 1980 and 1984 elections, Reagan won support from an average of 61 percent of the white working class, while his Democratic opponents, Carter and Walter Mondale, won an average of 35 percent.

Democrats would never fully recover their reputation as protectors of the average American, and they would lose any residual public support for the Keynesian view of government and the economy that left-leaning economists had championed. New Deal economics rested on the assumption that government intervention in the market could benefit workers and consumers. The Democrats' failures during the Carter years strengthened the traditional American argument, which Reagan voiced, that "in this present crisis, government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem." Government bureaucrats rather than corporate chieftains became the targets of public wrath. In short, by the end of Carter's term, the New Deal's economic agenda and theory lay in ruins.

Reagan avoided explicit racial appeals in constructing his coalition and focused on broader economic and cultural messages. But in some regions of the country, his coalition consisted of elements from the Nixon and George Wallace coalitions, and it owed its scope and scale largely to a southern political realignment in which race was certainly a factor. Reagan's county-by-county transformation of areas of the South that had been Democratic since before the Civil War into Republican strongholds was extraordinary. But Reagan achieved similar feats in northern working-class areas, where economic issues and a loss of confidence in the Democrats' ability to govern and lead America through the Cold War primarily drove the transformation.

Pennsylvania, a key pillar of the Democrats' New Deal coalition, told this story particularly clearly. Working-class counties like Carbon, Erie, Luzerne, and Northampton—which had voted for Democrat Hubert Humphry in 1968 and showed no interest in Wallace—swung toward Reagan in 1980 and remained Republican into the 1990s. But so did Montgomery County, outside Philadelphia, which was far from working-class and had been in the upscale and moderate Republican coalition of the early postwar era.

Reagan kept the bulk of the old Republican coalition, even though its voters were somewhat uncomfortable with his social conservatism. And he managed to add to it not only a significant new white working-class component but also numerous socially conservative evangelical Christians and Catholics who opposed abortion and were put off by the Democrats' counterculturalism.

But Reagan's primary political base was in the Sunbelt—those states stretching from the Carolinas and Florida through Texas, Arizona, and California. Republican dominance in the South became unquestionable: Running against a southern governor in 1980, Reagan won 61 percent of southern votes. In 1984, running against a northerner with even less appeal south of the Mason-Dixon Line, Reagan won 71 percent of southerners' votes.

Building and sustaining this coalition required Reagan and the Republicans to integrate and modulate a variety of ideological voices. There was much continuity between Reagan and Barry Goldwater—the badly defeated Republican presidential candidate in 1964. But the discontinuities made Reagan's coalition possible: He did not rail against the core New Deal programs, like Social Security or the minimum wage. Instead, he criticized the Great Society and focused on denouncing its least popular elements—affirmative action and welfare without work requirements—but not Medicare and Medicaid.

Combined with Reagan's defense of family, religion, and tradition, this modulated conservatism appealed to white working-class voters, sounding much more like Roosevelt than Hoover. Reagan defended a way of life that working-class voters valued while championing economic policies intended to control inflation, reduce the cost of living, and grow the economy.

Reagan's varied coalition partners did not agree about everything—far from it. On paper, they did not obviously belong together. But such tensions have marked every durable majority coalition in American history. Reagan drew together the elements of his coalition with a vision and agenda that spoke to some key concerns of each and avoided needlessly alienating any.

The Reagan coalition's logic persisted beyond Reagan's personal appeal and helped his much less charismatic vice president, George H. W. Bush, win a decisive victory in 1988. In that victory, too, Republicans relied on white working-class voters (winning roughly 60 percent of their votes). But Bush's grasp of these voters' concerns ultimately did not match Reagan's, and when confronted with an economic downturn, he did not prioritize them.

Ultimately, not one but two opponents perceived Bush's weakness with white working-class voters and brought him down. Ross Perot, an independent challenger who appealed especially to working-class Reagan voters, homed in on working-class concerns and systematically distinguished them from the priorities of Republicans in Washington. He opposed the Republicans' tax cuts, criticized them for neglecting to control spending, generally rejected their social conservatism, and accused them of having an excessively aggressive foreign policy—arguments perfectly suited to deconstructing the Reagan coalition and stealing its marginal voters. Perot won 19 percent of the popular vote in 1992, and more than two-thirds of his voters said they had voted for Bush four years earlier.

But Perot's fifth of the popular vote did not earn him a single electoral vote. He was effectively a breakaway Republican, and Republicans had a clear path to reclaim his voters by following his lead toward populism. Indeed, congressional Republicans led by Newt Gingrich arguably did this a few years later, winning control of both houses of Congress for the first time in four decades. But the direct beneficiary of Perot's deconstruction of the Reagan coalition was the Democratic candidate, Bill Clinton, who was acutely aware of Bush's vulnerabilities among working-class voters.

Drawing on a decade of work by a group of relatively centrist Democratic strategists and politicians, Clinton carefully bridged key elements of the Democratic coalition's progressive cultural agenda with an appeal to economic populism and cost-of-living issues. He did not re-create the New Deal coalition's connection with working-class voters, but he improved the Democrats' performance with those voters significantly.

Yet although he sketched a potential new Democratic majority and won the presidency, Clinton did not actually create a durable national majority coalition. He won 43 percent of the vote in 1992 and attained the presidency because of Perot's unusually strong independent performance. Republican victories in the 1994 congressional election immediately called the Clinton coalition's logic into question. And although Clinton was fairly decisively reelected in 1996 (winning 49 percent of the popular vote and 379 electoral votes), during the mid-1990s, a new phase of American politics began to take shape—a phase in which there has been no clear majority coalition.

The Reagan coalition was a thing of the past, and a Republican majority could no longer be taken for granted. Clinton's successes suggested how a Democratic majority could look. But seizing the opportunity to assemble it would require the Democratic Party to read the signs correctly, moderate its extremes, and appeal to working-class voters—as every successful coalition in modern American history has done. This was no sure thing, as soon became apparent.

## V. The Emerging Democratic Majority

The Democrats' rocky road started when Al Gore, the party's 2000 nominee, failed to replicate Clinton's successful model. While Gore narrowly won the popular vote (48.4 percent to George W. Bush's 47.9 percent), he narrowly lost the Electoral College vote (267 to 271), at least once the knife-edge outcome in Florida was decided. Gore's 267 electoral votes represented states that Clinton had carried twice. They included California (by an easy 12-point margin), Oregon, and Washington in the Pacific Northwest; New Mexico in the Southwest; Illinois; New Jersey (which Republicans had carried in every presidential election between 1968 and 1988); and every New England state but New Hampshire. With Gore's wins, the Democrats had now carried these states for three elections in a row. They were the Democrats' new base and showed how the geographic strength of the Democratic coalition had shifted.

On the other hand, Gore lost several Sunbelt states that Clinton had carried (Arizona, Florida, Nevada, and New Mexico), indicating that Democrats had a tenuous hold on states in that area, despite their rapidly changing demographics. In addition, Gore lost Ohio, which Clinton had won twice, suggesting Democratic vulnerability in the Rust Belt.

Gore's performance was generally strong among the Democrats' emerging constituencies, indicating that these constituencies were consolidating in the Democratic Party. He received 75 percent of the minority vote (which was about a fifth of the vote in 2000 and growing) and actually outperformed Clinton among blacks and Asians. Gore carried professionals by 7 points and women by 11 points.

Moreover, Gore did particularly well among the fastest-growing subcategories of women. Among single, working women—who had grown from 19 percent of the adult female population in 1970 to 29 percent in 2000—67 percent backed Gore, while 29 percent backed Bush. Most college-educated women—who had grown from just 8 percent of the 25-and-older female population to 24 percent—backed Gore, too, giving him 57 percent of their vote and Bush 39 percent.

But Gore severely underperformed relative to Clinton among white working-class voters. He lost them by 17 points, whereas Clinton had carried them by 1 point in 1996. Gore's deficit was a walloping 34 points among white working-class men.

The next Democratic presidential candidate, John Kerry, also fared well among emerging constituencies and carried roughly the same states. But he performed even worse among white working-class voters, losing them by a whopping 23 points in 2004. One could reasonably ascribe the Democrats' worsening deficit in 2004 to concerns about national security and terrorism after 9/11, which Republicans under Bush adeptly exploited. But this factor cannot explain the Democrats' sizable deficit among these voters in 2000. Thus, despite the success of Clinton's approach in the 1990s, serious Democratic weaknesses remained and manifested themselves both demographically and geographically. If Democrats were to develop a dominant majority coalition, they needed a better formula.

That formula had to address two key challenges. One, which promised big payoffs, was to take better advantage of ongoing demographic change, particularly in the rapidly changing Sunbelt, and successfully mobilize it. The other was to reverse the Democrats' bleeding among white working-class voters, which undermined the party's performance everywhere and threatened to undermine Democratic performance in Rust Belt states, where such voters were demographically dominant by far.

Barack Obama's 2008 coalition seemed to represent a successful new formula for a Democratic majority coalition along just these lines. Besides carrying all the states Kerry had won in 2004, Obama carried Colorado, Nevada, and New Mexico in the Southwest. In the South, he won Florida, North Carolina, and Virginia (flipping it toward the Democratic presidential candidate for the first time since 1964). Finally, he swept the Midwest, adding Iowa, Ohio, and even Indiana to Michigan, Minnesota, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin. Democrats also gained 21 seats in the House and eight seats in the Senate.

Obama's resounding victory was traceable to solving the Democrats' two key electoral challenges just mentioned: performing significantly better among emerging demographics to take full advantage of their rising share of the electorate and reversing the party's negative trend among white working-class voters. Among rising demographics, according to the big-data firm Catalist, Obama received support from 79 percent of non-white voters, carrying blacks by 88 points, Hispanics by 32 points, and Asians by 22 points. He also carried women by 16 points, single women by 35 points, and voters under age 30 by 30 points. Finally, among the burgeoning ranks of white college-educated voters, Obama made a historic breakthrough: He carried them by a point, including white college-educated women by 9 points.

Obama combined this strong performance among rising demographics with a decisive stop to the Democrats' bleeding among white working-class voters. He not only stemmed further losses but actually benefited from a Democratic surge among these voters, cutting his party's white working-class deficit to a comparatively modest 13 points. This surge appeared in many states that Obama carried.

In the Midwest, according to exit poll data (Catalist state-level data are not available for this election), Obama flipped white working-class voters in Iowa from a 2-point Democratic deficit to a 5-point Democratic advantage, in Michigan from a 5-point deficit to a 6-point advantage, and in Wisconsin from an 8-point deficit to a 5-point advantage.

In several states that Obama carried but Kerry hadn't, Obama did not succeed in carrying white working-class voters but drastically reduced the party's deficits among them. In Colorado, Obama roughly halved Kerry's 30-point deficit among these voters. He did the same with Kerry's 60-point deficit in North Carolina.

As it turned out, the 2008 election was the high point for the Democratic coalition that had risen from the New Deal coalition's ashes. Over the next two years, the Obama administration had great difficulty pulling the country out of the Great Recession. While the nation avoided the worst, its slow recovery from the crisis fed public doubts about Democratic economic management and spending priorities. A common perception that the Obama administration deferred to big financial interests at the expense of average voters gave these doubts a hard political edge.

In addition, the administration's biggest domestic reform, the Affordable Care Act (ACA), cost enormous political capital, while its benefits (to the extent they eventually emerged at all) were not immediately obvious to many voters. As a result, the bill was initially unpopular and did not help the Democrats politically; in fact, it did quite the contrary.

These developments set up the Democrats for an unusually bad midterm election in 2010. Midterm elections are normally bad for the incumbent party, but the Democrats' drubbing went far beyond expectations. Apparently, Obama's coalition was highly unstable and far from a durable, effective political formula.

The scale of the carnage for the Democrats in 2010 was astonishing. They lost 63 seats in the House, which flipped from Democratic control (256 vs. 179 seats) to Republican control (242 vs. 193 seats). Democrats lost the popular vote by 7 points in the House, inverting Obama's 7-point lead in the 2008 presidential election.

The Republicans gained House seats across the country, but ominously, Democratic strength in the upper Midwest was radically attenuated. House delegations in Illinois, Indiana, Michigan, Ohio, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin—all states Obama won—flipped from majority Democratic to majority Republican. While data on the demographics of state congressional voting are sparse, Catalist data indicate that Democratic congressional candidates' support from white working-class voters in Wisconsin fell by 26 margin points—from 6 points pro-Democratic in 2008 to 20 points pro-Republican in 2010 (in the two-party vote).

The Democrats also lost six Senate seats, chiefly in the Midwest (Illinois, Indiana, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin) and a net of six governorships (five in the Midwest: Iowa, Michigan, Ohio, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin). They also lost net 680 state legislative seats, with Democratic control of state legislative chambers dropping from a solid majority (61 Democratic to 36 Republican) to a weak minority (60 Republican to 36 Democratic).

While Democrats' support fell between 2008 and 2010 across virtually all demographics—including all the growing demographics Democrats had recently mobilized so well—the most consequential shift by far was among white working-class voters. They abandoned the party in droves, with the Democratic deficit among these voters ballooning to 23 points. At the time, the white working class still made up close to half of all voters—and a much greater portion in the upper Midwest, where a good share of the carnage took place.

Naturally, this worried the Democrats enormously as they looked toward the 2012 election and considered their need to reelect President Obama. Could they recover enough ground to hold the presidency and the Senate?

By 2012, the economy had improved significantly, though unemployment was still quite elevated—around 8 percent. The slow recovery ensured that, while the national mood was better, it was by no means "morning again in America" as far as voters were concerned. The Great Recession's damage still ran deep.

In light of this and the Republicans' nomination of a candidate—Mitt Romney—tied to private equity, Obama chose to run not on "hope and change," as he had done in 2008, but rather as a Democrat with a populist edge. As Nate Cohn pointed out:

Mr. Obama and his campaign team portrayed Mr. Romney as a plutocrat who dismantled companies and outsourced jobs. The implication was that he would leave middle-class jobs prey to globalization and corporations.

The proof of Mr. Obama's commitment to the working class and Mr. Romney's callousness, according to the Obama campaign, was the auto bailout: Mr. Obama protected the auto industry; Mr. Romney wrote "Let Detroit Go Bankrupt" in The New York Times.

This strategy, incumbent advantage, and a reservoir of goodwill among Obama's most supportive constituencies gave him a second victory. He became the first Democrat since Roosevelt—and the only president since Reagan—to win two consecutive elections with more than 50 percent of the popular vote.

Obama's electoral vote advantage was smaller than in 2008 (365 to 173) but still solid (332 to 206). Crucially, despite the 2010 carnage in the Midwest and Rust Belt, Obama managed to hold all the states he had won in that region in 2008 save Indiana, albeit with reduced margins. The only other state he lost from his 2008 coalition was North Carolina. Democrats made modest gains in the Senate (two seats), House (eight seats), and state legislatures (flipping seven chambers).

As was widely noted at the time, Obama recovered the ground he lost with non-white voters in 2010 and then some. Among blacks, Hispanics, and Asians, his margins exceeded the already-strong margins he had attained in 2008. The black turnout was good, slightly higher than in 2008. Less widely noted was that Obama moved the needle back significantly on Democrats' deficits with white working-class voters, regaining some of the ground lost in 2010.

For example, in Wisconsin, Obama gained back 18 margin points among these voters, approaching an even split and erasing most of the catastrophic drop Democrats had experienced among that state's white working class in 2010. Similarly, in Iowa, Obama managed to evenly split white working-class voters after Democrats had lost them by 16 points in 2010.

Obama's first achievement—his stellar performance among non-whites and the "rising American electorate"—was widely lauded in the press, among Democrats, and even among establishment Republicans. (Think of the Republican National Committee's post-2012 "autopsy" of the GOP defeat, which concentrated heavily on Democrats' success among Hispanics.) His second achievement—recovering so much ground among white working-class voters and holding the Midwest after it seemed to be slipping away from the Democrats—received far less attention and was essentially ignored in many Democratic circles.

This oversight proved to be a catastrophic error. Democratic elites believed the most important way to build their coalition was to further appeal to the rising American electorate, which was generally defined by the advocacy groups, nonprofits, and activists that loomed increasingly large within the party. If the party elites did not ignore voters in left-behind communities and demographics, they regarded them with suspicion and considered them probably infected with racism and hostility to a multicultural America.

Thus, the Obama administration's attention turned to issues like gun control and immigration. Notably, 2013 marked the beginning of Black Lives Matter (BLM), which coalesced around the shooting of Trayvon Martin in Miami Gardens, Florida, and which President Obama and most Democrats immediately embraced. But the movement came into its own with the 2014 death of Michael Brown in Ferguson, Missouri, which was followed by long, intense protests and significant violence. This drew a great deal of attention and support among Democrats for BLM and its claims about "systemic racism," despite the later exoneration of the policeman involved in Brown's death.

These developments blinded the Democrats to political factors that would undercut their appeal in 2014 and beyond. Three main factors came into play. The first was the ACA's unpopularity—as many working Americans found that their premiums were rising and that they could not (as they had been promised) keep the doctors they wanted.

The second factor was the persistent economic downturn. Unemployment had dropped since its peak in 2010, but it was still over 6 percent for most of 2014. Obama's failure to secure a larger stimulus program and his acquiescence to budget cuts after 2009 had subverted the recovery. In 2013, in the wake of spending cuts, the gross domestic product rose an anemic 1.8 percent.

The third factor was that—unlike in 2010, when the Republicans had nominated extreme right-wing Senate candidates who lost—this time, they nominated mainstream conservatives with much broader appeal, like Colorado's Cory Gardner for senator and Maryland's Larry Hogan for governor. The 2014 election hammered the final nail into the coffin of the thesis that the Democrats had an inevitable majority. It didn't show that the Republicans had acquired a permanent majority, but it demonstrated that the Democrats could not assume political history was on their side. American politics was caught in a seesaw, which would become even more apparent in subsequent elections.

Despite some economic improvement that election year—the unemployment rate finally dipped below 6 percent—the 2014 election, reflecting these three political factors, was still rough for the Democrats. The party lost 13 seats in the House and a stunning nine seats in the Senate (including many of the remaining Democratic seats in red states), flipping the chamber to the GOP to match Republican control of the House. Republicans gained two gubernatorial seats (for a total of 31) and attained control of another 10 state legislatures (67 total).

Voting patterns in the election foreshadowed future Democratic problems. Across demographics, the Democrats lost most of their 2012 gains in 2014. Particularly noticeable was the party's slipping performance among white working-class voters. Its national deficit among these voters went up to 26 points. In Wisconsin, white working-class support for the Democrats subsided almost to its levels in the 2010 wipeout election.

The next two years witnessed continued economic progress as the unemployment rate dipped below 5 percent by 2016 and steady economic growth continued. The Democrats were optimistic that, with the improved economy and Obama's rising approval rating, they could replicate Obama's 2012 coalition and secure a third straight presidential victory.

But that was not to be. Instead, the country entered a polarized era marked by surging populism and a brutal stalemate between the parties, with neither apparently capable of (or perhaps even interested in) forming a broad majority coalition.

## VI. The Donald Trump Dynamic

As the 2016 campaign unfolded, Democrats' optimism was enhanced by Donald Trump's victory in the Republican nomination contest. Due to Trump's profound character failings and seeming inability to avoid making offensive comments, most Democrats assumed he was making himself unelectable. Surely such a buffoon could not pull enough votes to win the election, especially since the country's shifting demographics were boosting the electoral weight of the very groups Trump was so intent on disparaging.

National polling data reinforced this view by showing Hillary Clinton persistently ahead, sometimes by wide margins, and solidly ahead in enough states to easily win the presidency. Near Election Day, forecasting-model estimates favored Clinton, assigning her a 70 percent to over 90 percent chance of victory.

Intertwined with what we know in retrospect were excessively rosy views of Clinton's chances, two fateful decisions by her campaign firmly undercut her ability to beat Trump. One occurred in the primaries, when Clinton faced a stiffer challenge than expected from Sen. Bernie Sanders of Vermont. Besides being more moderate than Sanders on policy, Clinton countered his class-oriented populist economics by flanking him to the left on identity politics. This built on the party's misinterpretation that simply mobilizing the rising American electorate had enabled Obama's 2012 victory.

After Sanders unexpectedly almost tied with Clinton in the Iowa caucus, she went on the offensive by characterizing Sanders's class-oriented pitch as racist and sexist. She continued that line of attack until she secured the nomination.

Next, with her campaign launched in earnest, Clinton made her second fateful decision: Given the reigning Democratic theory regarding 2012, her successful defeat of Sanders, and Trump's truly awful record on all the issues she had (mostly unfairly) criticized Sanders about, the obvious campaign strategy was to concentrate on Trump's character and how he was out of step with the rising American electorate.

Clinton duly adopted this strategy. Studies of her campaign ad spending reveal that the overwhelming majority of her ads said nothing about policy or even policy orientation but rather attacked Trump's character and many divisive, offensive statements. Clinton's campaign slogan, "stronger together," was an implicit rebuke of Trump on these grounds. Trump's ads, on the other hand, talked a great deal about policy—albeit in broad strokes—on issues like trade, immigration, and the elites' betrayal of the public.

Clinton's strategy seemed to work up to Election Day, despite signs of softening support in Rust Belt states (though the polls, we now know, were overestimating her support base). The campaign did not believe Clinton could lose to a candidate who was so clearly on the wrong side of history.

But lose she did. While carrying the popular vote by 2 percentage points, Clinton lost three states—Florida, Iowa, and Ohio—that Obama had carried twice and, crucially, narrowly lost three Rust Belt states—Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin—in the "blue wall" that the Democrats had carried in every presidential election since 1992. That made a 306-to-232 Electoral College victory for Trump (before eliminating faithless electors), despite Clinton's lead in the popular vote.

While Democrats gained six House seats and two Senate seats, they fell short of flipping either chamber, giving the GOP trifecta control of the federal government (i.e., control of the presidency, House, and Senate). The GOP also gained two state government trifectas, giving it complete control of 25 state governments, compared to a mere six for the Democrats.

The Democrats had anticipated a wave election for their party that would capitalize on Trump's presumed unpopularity and consolidate their power at all levels of government. Instead, the Republicans had one.

Trump's victory was attributable, above all, to the shift of white working-class voters, including many who had voted for Obama, into the Republican column. In the country as a whole, the Republican advantage among white working-class voters rose 6 points to a staggering margin of 31 points. White college-educated voters went in exactly the opposite direction, increasing their support for the Democrats by 6 points.

But white working-class voters are far more numerous than their college-educated counterparts, particularly in key areas of the country like the Midwest and Rust Belt. And it was here that the Republicans struck their death blow. In Iowa and Ohio, where Clinton got blown out, white working-class voters moved, respectively, 22 and 15 margin points toward the GOP. And in Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin, where Trump's advantages were narrow, Democratic support from the white working class declined by 11, 8, and 13 points, respectively.

These shifts were large and decisive. Simulations show that if Clinton's white working-class support had matched Obama's in 2012, she would have carried all these states easily. Indeed, if Clinton had simply managed to reduce her losses among these voters by a quarter, she would have been elected president.

As analysts sifted through the wreckage of Democratic performance trying to understand where all the Trump votes had come from, some themes began to emerge. Across county-level studies, low educational levels among whites were clearly a robust predictor of shifts toward Trump. These studies also indicated that counties that swung toward Trump tended to depend on low-skill jobs, perform relatively poorly by a range of economic measures, and have local economies that were particularly vulnerable to automation and offshoring. Finally, there was strong evidence that Trump-swinging counties tended to be literally sick, in that their inhabitants had relatively poor physical health and high mortality due to alcoholism, drug abuse, and suicide.

The picture was more complicated when considering the individual-level characteristics of Trump voters, and especially of those who had voted for Obama but then voted for Trump. These included some aspects of economic populism—opposition to cutting Social Security and Medicare, suspicion of free trade and trade agreements, and taxing the rich—and traditional populist attitudes like anti-elitism and mistrust of experts. But a lot of the post-election analysis focused on another variable in place of these—a variable that political scientists labeled "racial resentment" and that many studies revealed was strongly correlated with voting Republican in the 2016 presidential election.

This variable is measured by a scale created from statements like: "Irish, Italians, Jews, and many other minorities overcame prejudice and worked their way up. Blacks should do the same without any special favors." Political scientists often and uncritically employ this variable to indicate racial animus, although these statements obviously correspond closely to a generic conservative view of avenues to social mobility. Indeed, political scientists Riley Carney and Ryan Enos showed that responses to statements like these change little if you substitute "Nepalese" or "Lithuanians" for "blacks." That implies the statements that make up the scale tap views not explicitly about blacks. Carney and Enos term these views "just world belief," which sounds quite different from "racial resentment."

But in the aftermath of the Trump election, researchers continued to use the same scale with the same name and interpretation and no caveats. The variable's strong relationship to voting for Trump proved, they argued, that support for Trump, including vote switching from Obama to Trump, was driven fundamentally by underlying racism and xenophobia. Imagine, though, what these studies might have concluded if they had tied supporting Trump to activating just world belief, an eminently reasonable interpretation of their star variable, instead of racial resentment. Their lack of any interest in exploring this alternative interpretation strongly suggests that the researchers' political beliefs were influencing how they chose to pursue and present their studies. In short, they went looking for racism and found it.

Other studies played variations on this theme, adding variables related to immigration and even trade to the mix and presuming that negative views showed "status threat" or some other euphemism for racism or xenophobia. As sociologist Stephen Morgan noted in a series of papers, this amounts to a labeling exercise that strips issues with a clear economic component of that component and reduces them to simple indicators of unenlightened social attitudes. Again, researchers' prior views and political beliefs were influencing their analysis and interpretation of results.

There is an even deeper problem with the conventional view that racial resentment drove votes for Trump. Start with a fact most studies glossed over or ignored: Trends in so-called racial resentment went in the "wrong" direction between the 2012 and 2016 elections. That is, fewer whites had high levels of racial resentment in 2016 than in 2012. This makes racial resentment an odd means to explain white voters' shift toward Trump in the 2016 election.

Political scientists Justin Grimmer and William Marble investigated this conundrum intensively by directly examining whether an indicator like racial resentment could explain the shift of millions of white votes toward Trump. The studies that gave pride of place to racial resentment in explaining Trump's victory did no such accounting; the researchers simply showed a strong relationship between the variable they defined as racial resentment and Republican voting in 2016 and thought they'd provided a complete explanation.

They had not. When you look at the voter population and how racial resentment was distributed in 2016, as Grimmer and Marble did, it turns out that racial resentment simply does not explain voter shifts. A rigorous accounting of shifts toward Trump shows instead that they occurred primarily among whites—especially less educated whites—with moderate views on race and immigration, not whites with high levels of racial resentment. In fact, Trump actually netted fewer votes in 2016 among whites with high levels of "racial resentment" than Romney did in 2012.

Clearly, a more complex explanation for Trump's victory was in order, one integrating negative views on immigration, trade, and liberal elites with a sense of unfairness. That would have helped Democrats understand why, in counties that shifted toward Trump, voters whose ways of life were being torn asunder by economic and social change were so attracted to Trump's appeals.

Such an understanding was nowhere to be found, however, in Democratic ranks. The racism-and-xenophobia interpretation quickly dominated, partly because it continued the approach Clinton had taken during her campaign, which most Democrats accepted. Indeed, this interpretation became so dominant that simply questioning it reliably opened the questioner to accusations of failing to take the problem of racism seriously enough.

Between 2016 and 2018, Trump's actions in office, and particularly his rhetoric, provided plenty of material that seemed to confirm the dominant interpretation: Trump and Trumpism were simply about racism and xenophobia and had no other content. This encouraged the Democratic Party's burgeoning cultural left to build on that interpretation and link it to their radical critique of American society as structurally racist, hostile to all "marginalized" communities, and embedded within a rapacious capitalism that would destroy the planet.

This view spread through sympathetic cultural milieus where it already had a considerable presence: universities, media, the arts, nonprofits, advocacy groups, foundations, and the Democratic Party's infrastructure. In those spaces, progressivism became redefined to include that radical critique and the necessary link to all associated issues. In intense opposition to Trump, a certain radicalism seemed only natural.

But that was not true outside these milieus, where moderate-to-liberal voters wanted simply to foil the Republicans and get rid of Trump, whom they found profoundly distasteful. These voters provided the shock troops for the #Resistance and powered the defeat of Republican candidates in 2018. Such voters were not particularly interested in promoting a radical critique of American society and certainly didn't think their organizing for Democratic candidates represented much more than a desire to beat Trump and the candidates who supported him.

The result was a successful 2018 election for the Democrats, who took full advantage of Trump's unpopularity (his approval rating at election time was around 42 percent) and the outrage among not just liberal but also moderate voters at his untethered rhetoric and actions. In a historically high-turnout off-year election, Democrats gained net 41 House seats and carried the House popular vote by almost 9 points. Those gains flipped the Republicans' 241-to-194 House majority to a 235-to-199 Democratic one.

Democrats gained seven governorships, including in the swing states of Michigan, Nevada, and Wisconsin; 350 state legislative seats; and control of seven state legislative chambers. But Republicans retained control of the US Senate, gaining two seats over 2016. This happened because they took out four Democratic incumbents in red or reddish states (Florida, Indiana, Missouri, and North Dakota), outweighing the Democrats' two flips of Republican seats in swing states (Arizona and Nevada).

Media analysis of the 2018 election results tended to emphasize shifts among suburban and educated voters toward the Democrats relative to 2016 and high turnout among these constituencies. It is true that these voters turned out in high numbers, and some shifted strongly toward the Democrats. But a detailed postelection analysis of voter file and other data paints a much more complex—and interesting—picture.

To begin with, the shift toward the Democrats was larger in rural than suburban areas and among rural than suburban whites. There were also big pro-Democratic shifts among both noncollege- and college-educated white voters—each about 7 margin points.

Moreover, despite the stellar election turnout (for a midterm year) and a big surge in new voters, the Democrats' improved 2018 performance mainly did not come from this higher turnout. Rather, it came overwhelmingly from voters who voted in both elections and switched their votes from Republican in 2016 to Democratic in 2018. Specifically, Catalist estimated that around 90 percent of the Democrats' improved performance in 2018 came from persuasion—vote switchers—not increased turnout.

The 2018 election showed promise for the Democrats in the three Rust Belt states that had delivered Trump's 2016 victory: Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin. Adding Minnesota, which Trump lost by less than 2 points, Catalist data showed that Democratic victories in these states were overwhelmingly driven by white voters' shifts away from 2016 support of Trump. The vote shifted toward Democrats in 2018 by 15 points in Senate elections, 13 points in gubernatorial elections, and 9 points in House elections. Interestingly, these shifts were considerably larger than shifts toward the Democrats among non-white voters, which tended to be in the low single digits.

Crucially, given the Democrats' key weakness in 2016, pro-Democratic shifts were substantial among white noncollege-educated voters in these states in 2018—13 points in Senate elections, 11 points in gubernatorial elections, and 5 points in House elections. Shifts were even larger among white college-educated voters, though these states contained a markedly smaller proportion of white voters (around a third smaller).

Another key aspect of the 2018 election was the particular Democratic candidates who were responsible for their party's gain of 41 House seats. These Democrats did not embrace the emerging cultural radicalism or, indeed, any kind of radicalism. Instead, they overwhelmingly ran on health care, targeting especially the GOP's attempts to eliminate popular Obamacare policies like protecting people with preexisting conditions. Secondarily, they attacked the GOP Congress's big tax bill, which they insisted provided tax cuts primarily to the affluent. These candidates generally implied criticism of Trump rather than stating it explicitly.

But the dominant interpretation of the election in Democratic circles ignored much of this. Instead of highlighting the party's success in persuading many voters to switch sides, this interpretation looked at the high turnout of Democratic-leaning groups and credited the mobilization energies unleashed by the party's rising left wing with the victory.

Greatly assisting this emerging conventional wisdom was the media's bizarre obsession with four new Democratic House members (all non-white women) elected by deep-blue urban districts, two of whom—Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D-NY) and Rep. Ayanna Pressley (D-MA)—won their party's nomination by defeating liberal incumbents in primaries. None of these seats represented gains for the Democrats, but "the Squad" captivated the press and supposedly encapsulated the youthful radical energies that flowed through the party and had powered its 2018 victories.

## VII. The Joe Biden Dynamic

This utterly fanciful story made it extremely difficult for Democrats to draw useful lessons from the 2018 election, instead encouraging a sense that the party must respond to this youthful radical energy by absorbing its cultural outlook and maximalist politics. By the time the first Democratic presidential debates were held in late June 2019, leading candidates who aspired to take on Trump were aggressively positioning themselves within this presumed left-wing consensus and seeking to "out-left" each other.

Many of these candidates endorsed a wide range of radical policies: Medicare for All reforms that would eliminate private health insurance, a Green New Deal with an aggressive timeline for elimination of fossil fuels, bans on fracking, decriminalization of illegal migration over the Mexican border, health insurance for illegal immigrants, allowing prisoners to vote, the abolition of US Immigration and Customs Enforcement, and much more.

While candidates for the Democratic nomination were floating such ideas, the latter half of Trump's term became ever more polarizing, leading the newly Democratic House to impeach him on December 18, 2019. Shortly thereafter, the COVID-19 pandemic hit the United States, leading to massive nationwide shutdowns starting in March 2020, followed by a sharp economic contraction and skyrocketing unemployment. Meanwhile, Trump continued roiling the waters with bizarre theories about the virus and how to treat it, starkly politicizing the public health emergency.

Joe Biden took advantage of his opponents' radical ideas and the reigning sense of chaos by striking a moderate note—promising to pursue progressive but sensible policies, get through the pandemic, and, of course and above all, beat Trump. This was a congenial message to the Democratic primary's electorate, from black voters in South Carolina to every Democratic demographic on Super Tuesday and beyond.

It turned out that, despite many candidates' strenuous appeals to the party's rising left, most voters in the Democratic primary had more pragmatic and moderate views than the media-anointed advocates of a radical party had. Other candidates' failure to understand this opened the field for Biden, who cruised to the nomination after Super Tuesday.

While Biden wrapped up his nomination, cultural radicalism (especially regarding race) continued to gather force in the party's left wing and among progressive elites. BLM became more active, responding to several events early that year, including the shooting of Breonna Taylor in Louisville, Kentucky, in March. But with the late May killing of George Floyd in Minneapolis, Minnesota, things really took off.

The killing sparked an unprecedented surge of protests, many of them violent, starting in Minneapolis and spreading nationwide. Remarkably, these protests' tenor and rhetoric went far beyond condemning an egregious act of police brutality to embracing, fully and uncritically, BLM's political philosophy. This profoundly radical philosophy viewed the United States as a white supremacist society and asserted that all white Americans were complicit in maintaining the racial hierarchy.

Nobody wanted to hear a discouraging word about the BLM protests during the summer after Floyd's killing—even when calls to "defund the police" arose and became popular in protest circles. This slogan, adjacent to and frequently embracing the cause of police and prison abolition, entered public discourse with only mild initial pushback from Democrats. There it was, painted in 35-foot-tall, bright-yellow block letters on 16th Street in Democratic-controlled Washington, DC (with a helpful "=" sign connecting it with DC Mayor Muriel Bowser's previous "Black Lives Matter" signage on the same street). The two messages' conjunction was indelibly seared into the nation's consciousness, and Democrats never managed to thoroughly dissociate from the politically toxic "defund the police" slogan (not to mention the protest violence and looting) while remaining attached to the BLM movement.

If anyone in the Biden campaign had qualms about how all this would affect the election, they took comfort from two facts. First, Biden was far ahead as summer turned to fall—leading by 8 points nationally in mid-September and 10 points in mid-October. The final forecast from FiveThirtyEight projected Biden would carry not only the "Rust Belt three" that had handed Trump the 2016 election (Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin) but also Arizona, Florida, Georgia, and North Carolina. Ohio was projected to be close—within a point—and Iowa and Texas within 2 points. Biden's projected victory margin was 8 points in Michigan and Wisconsin and 5 points in Pennsylvania. Democrats were also projected to take back the Senate, attaining a three- to four-seat margin, and to pad their lead in the House.

The second comforting aspect of the campaign was that the obvious contrast its operatives wanted to draw—between the disruptive, chaotic presidency of Trump, who could not manage the COVID-19 pandemic and economy, and the reassuring approach of moderate, trusted Biden, who would restore America to normality in both realms—appeared to take and define the contest. The stage seemed set for a resounding rejection of the incumbent administration and perhaps another wave election for the Democrats echoing their success in 2018.

This made Biden's decision to absorb the left wing of his party into his campaign look like a winning strategy, or at least a harmless one. A big victory seemed in the offing. But to the Democrats' great shock, this was not the election result they got. Once again, the polls had overestimated Democratic strength across the board.

Biden carried the national popular vote by a little over 4 points, better than Clinton's margin in 2016 but far from the gaudy margin the campaign and political pundits had anticipated. He carried Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin, but the results were much closer than projected: Michigan by less than 3 points, Pennsylvania by less than 2 points, and Wisconsin by under a point. Biden carried his breakthrough states, Arizona and Georgia, by razor-thin margins of three-tenths and one-fifth of a percentage point, respectively. And he got blown out by 8 points in Iowa and Ohio, which were supposed to be close.

Democrats, to their astonishment, lost 13 seats in the House, reducing their majority to a narrow 222-to-213 advantage. They managed to gain the Senate only by improbably winning two runoffs in Georgia, as Trump subverted his own party's electoral chances by suppressing turnout among his supporters. At the state level, Democrats fell further behind in governorships (a net loss of one, with 27 Republican and 23 Democratic governors) and control of state legislative chambers (a net loss of two, with 61 under Republican and 38 under Democratic control). This was hardly the wave election Democrats had anticipated.

Yet attaining trifecta control of the federal government, albeit by the thinnest margin, allowed most Democrats to ignore or downplay the election's many troubling signs. Beyond the disappointing topline results described above, the vote's internal demographic patterns and trends showed how tenuous the Democrats' victory was.

Because of his campaign's focus and his personality, Biden reduced Trump's national advantage from 2016 among white working-class voters by 2 points. This shift, though small, helped Biden carry the key Rust Belt states of Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin, but it was not enough to reverse Clinton's losses in Ohio and Iowa, which Obama had carried. Overall, Biden still lost white working-class voters by 26 points. Interviews with these voters found they especially appreciated Trump's trade policy.

Contrary to expectations, Biden did not benefit from increased support among non-white voters in 2020. His margin among these voters actually declined by 11 points compared with Clinton in 2016. This included a 6-point decline among blacks and, remarkably, an 18-point decline among Hispanic voters that was heavily concentrated among working-class voters.

There is no single explanation for Biden's and the Democrats' fading advantage among Hispanic voters. In Florida, the party's association with socialism—through Sanders and the renewal of relations with Cuba, which Obama and Biden facilitated—particularly hurt its support among Cubans and Venezuelan immigrants. In South Texas, the party suffered likewise from its association with socialism and from Tejano support for Trump's tough border policies, Democratic hostility to police and border agents, and Democratic support for eliminating oil and gas production.

In cities like Chicago, where Hispanic precincts shifted toward Trump, voters might have reacted to Democratic identification with black rioters and support for defunding the police, which Hispanic voters nationwide overwhelmingly opposed. Overall, too, many Hispanics seemingly felt the country had fared well under Trump before the pandemic and would do so again. Over half of Hispanic voters were very or somewhat confident that Trump could make good decisions about economic policy.

Biden mostly held his own (compared to Clinton in 2016) with Asian American voters, but some signs of trouble could be discerned in California's congressional races and local races. Like Hispanics, Asian voters were concerned about public safety and rejected demands to defund the police. Asian voters in California, New York, and Virginia were also upset by the Democrats' support for aggressive affirmative action at their expense. Since Asians were enrolled in high schools for gifted and talented students and top-tier colleges at percentages well above their percentage in the population, the quota systems Democrats were supporting would harm them. Partly in reaction to this, Asian neighborhoods in New York City swung by double digits toward Trump in 2020. In California, Asians and Hispanics played a large part in defeating an affirmative action referendum, which lost by 57 percent to 43 percent.

In all, the elections suggested that, while the Democrats had succeeded in 2020 where they had failed in 2016, this was largely because of voters' attention to Trump's misdeeds. If the Republicans had run their own version of Biden, the results might have been different. The Democrats had still not established their credibility as stewards of the economy. And the cultural radicalism of the Democrats' "shadow party"—the activists, think tanks, foundations, publications, websites, big donors, and prestigious intellectuals that are not officially in the party but influence and identify with it—endangered the party extremely among not only the general electorate but also less ideological Democrats. Biden had no mandate except not to be Trump.

## VIII. The 2022 Campaign

Throughout much of 2022, Democrats feared they would get routed in the midterm elections. Biden's approval ratings were about where Trump's had been before the 2018 midterms. But Biden didn't inspire the same visceral dislike as Trump. Instead, he was seen as doddering and ineffective. In focus groups, when undecided or swing voters (for instance, those who had backed Trump in 2016 but Biden in 2020) were asked to describe Biden in a word or phrase, they chose "old and slow," "old and needs to be stronger," "ineffective," "stuck in the past," "tucked away," and "lost his fastball."

Voters were sour that inflation was eating away at their savings and earnings. People were worried about rising crime rates, which they sometimes blamed on activists' demands to defund the police or eliminate cash bail, and many criticized Biden for failing to control the country's southern border.

But three factors improved Democratic prospects. First, in key Senate and governor races, the Republicans nominated candidates who were inexperienced, had been endorsed by Trump, and shared Trump's conviction that the 2020 election had been stolen. These candidates—like the 2010 Tea Party candidates—repelled potential Republican voters who were not Trump activists or hard-line partisans by conjuring images of chaos and disorder. Second, in June 2022, the Supreme Court (which now had a Republican-appointed majority) overturned Roe v. Wade, sparking battles over abortion in many states. These two factors allowed the Democrats to accuse the Republicans of extremism.

A third factor aiding the Democrats was that Biden and his party warded off charges that they couldn't get things done by passing two big bills, the bipartisan CHIPS and Science Act and the climate-oriented Inflation Reduction Act. Few members of the public knew these bills' details, but their passage dimmed the image of ineptitude that a chaotic withdrawal from Afghanistan had created. Biden's approval rating ticked upward on the eve of the election.

Together, these three factors turned what could have been a rout into a standoff. Democrats increased their functional margin in the Senate by one seat. Republicans won back the House, but narrowly, with a 222-to-213 margin. And Democrats gained two governorships.

As New York Times analyst Nate Cohn has argued, Democrats won crucial races whose major issues included "stop the steal" and abortion, such as in Michigan and Pennsylvania. But Democrats lost races in which voters' doubts about Biden or the party's cultural radicalism predominated instead, such as in Florida, New York, and Texas.

But as far as the Democratic Party's future was concerned, the midterm results provided little reassurance. Democratic success depended primarily on Republican extremism. The Democrats failed to stem or reverse their loss of working-class voters. In the overall House vote, the Republican margin among white working-class voters rose from 20 points in 2018 to 27 points in 2022, a 7-point swing against the Democrats.

The swing among non-white working-class voters was even larger, reducing the Democrats' advantage by 15 points between the two elections. Overall, the Democratic margin declined by 11 points among Hispanics, 6 points among blacks, and 19 points among Asians between 2018 and 2022. Democrats' losses among non-whites were about twice the size of their losses among whites, and their losses among non-white working-class voters were about twice the size of their losses among non-white college-educated voters.

In Wisconsin, an unpopular and vulnerable Republican incumbent, Sen. Ron Johnson, defeated his Democratic challenger by pointing to that challenger's support for defunding the police and eliminating cash bail.

In New York, the Democrats' identification with radicalism in criminal justice crippled their candidates, with the issue clearly contributing to the Democrats' stunning loss of four House seats. Statewide, there was a big vote shift toward Republicans relative to 2020, with Republican gubernatorial candidate Lee Zeldin running ahead of Trump by 17 points among black voters, 18 points among Hispanic voters, and 34 points among other non-white voters, who were mostly Asians.

Republicans swept Iowa—which Obama had won twice, whose congressional delegation had usually been equally divided between Democrats and Republicans, and whose statehouse periodically changed hands between parties—by campaigning against Democrats' support for defunding the police, inability to police the southern border, support for gun control, and radical views on gender and sex. Republicans won the top statewide offices, increased their margin in the state legislatures, reelected one of their two US senators, and, perhaps most tellingly, ousted the one remaining Democratic US House member.

Democrats' immigration policies continued to damage their chances in Texas, where Republicans once again easily swept all statewide offices. In the House elections, Democrats won the working-class Hispanic vote statewide by only 6 points. Republican Gov. Greg Abbott defeated former Rep. Beto O'Rourke (D-TX), running about 5 points ahead of Trump in 2022. He performed even better among non-white working-class voters, running 15 points ahead of Trump across the state.

Democrats' policies on sex and gender hurt them in Florida. Gov. Ron DeSantis, who strenuously opposed these policies, won reelection in a romp—by 19 points—while Florida House Republicans carried the statewide popular vote by 19 points and picked up three seats. Democrats had assumed Republicans' sponsorship of a law prohibiting instruction in gender ideology for K–3 children would hurt them politically, but the opposite seems true.

Democrats had also assumed that DeSantis's involvement in flying migrants to Martha's Vineyard would hurt him, especially with Hispanic voters. In fact, Floridian Hispanics approved of the Martha's Vineyard flights and the restriction on teaching gender ideology. That's one reason DeSantis cleaned up among Hispanic voters, carrying Miami-Dade County by 11 points and Hispanics statewide by 13 points. All told, DeSantis ran 16 points ahead of Trump in his 2022 gubernatorial race. Even more impressively, the governor ran 27 points ahead of Trump among non-white working-class voters. And he ran 38 points better among non-white working-class voters in 2022 than he did in his initial 2018 gubernatorial race.

All of this suggests that 2022 continued the trend of recent elections: Republicans gained working-class voters while losing college graduates, Democrats did the opposite, and neither has discovered how to grow its overall vote share substantially. While both coalitions are shifting under the surface, they remain deadlocked overall.

## IX. Another Stalemate Election?

The stalemate in American politics has only intensified since 2022; the era of no majorities seems likely to continue through the 2024 election.

Both parties during this period have typically been unwilling to learn from failure. They treat narrow victories like landslides and wave away narrow defeats, somehow seeing both as confirmation of their existing strategies. There is no clearer example of this logic than the Republican Party's decision to run Trump as its presidential nominee again in 2024—essentially setting up a rerun of an election it lost four years earlier. Republicans assumed that trying exactly the same thing could have the opposite result, and of course they were not obviously wrong. Their mistake was imagining that trying their luck in another 50–50 matchup was their only option—and assuming that working to significantly broaden their coalition was not realistic.

The Democrats seemed willing to repeat the same election, too, at least until President Biden's disastrous debate performance put his age-related decline front and center. Replacing Biden with Kamala Harris at the top of the Democrats' ticket may let them lay down the burdens of incumbency, but it does not suggest a fundamental reorientation; indeed, Harris remains weak among working-class voters, just as Biden was.

This period has also featured declining ideological diversity within the parties and a diminishment of significant intraparty factions. Each party has become more internally cohesive as the gap between them has grown more rigid. This has especially hampered the functioning of Congress, where intraparty factions are essential for cross-partisan bargaining and negotiations. It has also shaped presidential elections, narrowing the traditional differences in strategies between primary and general election campaigns.

Presidential contenders increasingly run as primary candidates all the way until the November election. Indeed, in 2024, the line between the primary and general elections seemed all but erased, as both parties had selected their candidates by the beginning of the spring, and there was no real primary season. Without much deliberation, the two campaigns agreed that the first general-election debate would occur on June 27, before either party had even held its convention. The Democrats, as noted, ended up replacing their presidential candidate shortly after that debate, but again without any real deliberation at their convention. The entire architecture of the parties' process for selecting presidential candidates is crumbling before our eyes.

In this era, both parties play the minority party's traditional role. Each party runs campaigns focused almost entirely on the faults of the other, with no serious strategy for significantly broadening its electoral reach. Each offers itself fundamentally as an alternative to the other, even if it has the incumbent majority in Congress or holds the White House. And neither offers a clear or broadly compelling vision of the country's future. This noxious stalemate has shaped an entire generation of American political life.

Can this stalemate be broken? It can, but that would require one party to go outside the comfort zone of appealing to its base supporters and aim directly at the broad center of American politics. In the next section, we document opportunities for coalition building with data from a proprietary 6,000-respondent survey that delves deeply into how most Americans' views line up (or don't) with those of party partisans on both sides.

## X. Coalitional Opportunities

NORC conducted our proprietary survey for AEI's Survey Center on American Life. The survey's size and detail allow us to take a granular look at each party's opportunities for expanding its coalition—and how its vulnerabilities, typically linked to strong partisans' preferences, have undercut those opportunities. Publicly available data provide additional support for our analysis.

### Republican Opportunities

The Republican coalition, as the above analysis shows, relies heavily on white working-class voters and has an emerging weakness among white college-educated voters. The GOP generally does poorly among non-white voters, especially blacks, but is starting to do better among non-white working-class voters.

Geographically, Republicans dominate rural and small-town America and do much better in outer suburbs and exurbs than in more educated, affluent inner suburbs. They get walloped in dense urban areas.

This coalition is adequate to hold the Democrats to a stalemate but not much else, and Republicans clearly cannot press electoral advantages when they emerge. Our analysis indicates that this is the case not because Republicans lack opportunities to break the stalemate but rather because they are unable, if not unwilling, to capitalize on those opportunities.

Here are Republicans' key opportunities.

Democratic Cultural Radicalism. Republican opportunities are most obvious here. Democrats have become associated with a wide variety of "leftist" minority views on cultural issues. These positions appeal to the activists and liberal college graduates who are influential within the party but much less so outside it.

Incontrovertibly, Republicans owe much of their success among white working-class voters to these voters' distaste for Democrats' cultural leftism. But there are openings for Republicans to expand their coalition to other demographics among whom negative reactions are common.

Immigration. When Biden came into office, he immediately issued executive orders dramatically loosening the rules for handling illegal immigrants. His party's left wing and various immigration advocacy groups rapturously applauded this. As the New York Times' David Leonhardt summarized:

Biden tried to pause deportations. He changed the definition of asylum to include fear of gang violence. He used immigration parole—which the law says should be used "on a case-by-case basis for urgent humanitarian reasons"—to admit hundreds of thousands of people. The parole programs alone amounted to "the largest expansion of legal immigration in modern U.S. history," Camilo Montoya-Galvez of CBS News wrote.

Would-be migrants, as well as the Mexican cartels that run transit networks, heard a clear message: Entering the United States had become easier. The number of people attempting to do so spiked almost immediately.

Another Times reporter, Miriam Jordan, crisply explained why so many migrants came:

It is not just because they believe they will be able to make it across the 2,000 mile southern frontier. They are also certain that once they make it to the United States they will be able to stay.

Forever.

And by and large, they are not wrong.

Predictably, this dramatic surge in illegal immigration and the immigrants' diffusion into overburdened cities countrywide caused a spike in the issue's salience and negative sentiment toward Biden and the Democrats for letting the situation get out of control. This has given Trump and the Republicans huge advantages in whom voters trust to handle the issue. These advantages have continued even after the Biden administration finally tightened the border in mid-2024 through executive action and despite Harris running commercials promising to be tough on border security.

The Democrats should have seen this coming. At the time of Biden's succession, border security was a huge issue in American public opinion and politics that any attempt to change or "humanize" the immigration system could not elide. This is even more true now. Public opinion polling over the years has consistently shown that overwhelming majorities favor more spending and emphasis on border security.

Therefore, even though the public had become more sympathetic to immigrants and immigration, partially in reaction to the Trump administration's practices, that did not mean Democrats could simply take a stance on this issue opposite to Trump's. Any increased leniency at the border raised the possibility of highly unpopular knock-on effects and unintended consequences, as outlined above. In fact, voters are increasingly open to immigration restrictions. An astonishing 62 percent of voters in a June CBS News survey supported "starting 'a new national program to deport all undocumented immigrants currently living in the US illegally,'" including 47 percent of blacks, 58 percent of white college graduates, and even 53 percent of Hispanics.

Other data indicate support for tough measures to clamp down on illegal immigration. In an April 2024 YouGov survey, voters supported "us[ing] existing presidential powers to stop illegal migrant crossings at the U.S.-Mexico border" by 53 points. (Biden finally and reluctantly adopted a mild version of this plan in June 2024.) They also supported "deputiz[ing] the National Guard and local law enforcement to assist with rapidly removing gang members and criminals living illegally in the United States" by 45 points, "restrict[ing] the ability of migrants who illegally cross the U.S.-Mexico border to seek asylum" by 39 points, and "build[ing] a full wall on the US-Mexico border" by 21 points. They even supported "chang[ing] federal law so that drug traffickers can receive the death penalty" by 20 points.

Apparently, voters think enough is enough; Republicans are well positioned to respond to these concerns by advocating and taking tough action at the border. This may allow them to expand their advantage among working-class voters, pick off more college-educated whites, and further erode Democrats' advantage among non-white voters.

However, that does not mean voters are opposed to immigration and immigrants, full stop. They are not. Gallup persistently finds that two-thirds to three-fourths of the public view immigration as good rather than bad for the country. (The share who hold this view is even higher if the question specifies legal immigration.) The same YouGov survey cited above asked voters to pick one of three approaches to immigration. By a wide margin, they preferred the middle of three border security levels plus better-managed legal immigration to help the economy.

Clearly, there is a sweet spot among voters for an approach that twins serious border security with clear and economically productive criteria for who can enter the country legally while eliminating opportunities to game the asylum system. But too often, Republicans seem simply opposed to immigration and interested only in making it harder to enter the country. That is not the way to capture the electorate's core.

Crime. In the aftermath of Floyd's killing by police and the nationwide movement that it sparked, the climate for police and criminal justice reform was highly favorable. But Democrats blew the opportunity by allowing their party to be associated with unpopular movement slogans, like "defund the police," that appeared not to take public safety concerns seriously.

At the same time, Democrats became associated with a wave of progressive public prosecutors who seemed hesitant about keeping criminals off the street, even as a spike in violent crimes, like murders and carjacking, swept the nation. This accompanied a climate of tolerance and non-prosecution for lesser crimes that degraded the quality of life in many Democratic-controlled cities. San Francisco became practically a poster child for the latter problem under District Attorney Chesa Boudin's "leadership."

So the voters kicked Boudin out in a recall election. Based on neighborhood voting patterns and preelection polling data, Asian voters' support for the recall seems to have been particularly strong. Such non-white support for cashiering Boudin shouldn't be surprising. The most enthusiastic supporters of Boudin's approach to policing tend to be white college-educated liberals. Voters who are non-white or working-class approach crime quite differently. Think of Eric Adams's and Cherelle Parker's support bases in their successful runs for mayor of New York and Philadelphia, respectively.

Such sentiments in pro-Democratic non-white and working-class urban areas should not be puzzling. These voters tend to live in areas with more crime and are therefore unlikely to look kindly on any approach that threatens public safety. A Pew Research Center poll found that black and Hispanic Democrats—who are far more likely than white Democrats to live in urban areas and belong to the working class—are significantly more likely than white Democrats to favor more police funding in their area.

Our survey confirms the strength of these sentiments. We asked about police funding in two different ways. Respondents were more likely to endorse fully funding police departments over reallocating police funding to social services (65 percent vs. 34 percent). They were also more likely to back keeping police budgets whole in the interest of public safety over reducing them and transferring the money to social services (73 percent vs. 25 percent).

Crucially, non-white working-class voters—among whom Democrats have steadily lost ground—opposed reducing police budgets by a 30-point margin. That margin ballooned to 50 points among moderate-to-conservative voters within this demographic, who comprise its overwhelming majority. By contrast, white, liberal college graduates—whose priorities have increasingly defined the Democratic Party—favored reducing police budgets by a 20-point margin.

No wonder Republicans enjoy a wide advantage on the issue of crime and public safety—which continues even as Harris attempts to leverage her history as a prosecutor to project a "tough on crime" image. In a November 2023 Democracy Corps survey of battleground states and districts, voters favored Trump over Biden by 17 points on "handling crime." The survey also asked these voters what their biggest concern would be if the Democratic candidate won the election. Top concerns involved an open border, but just a few points behind was "crime and homelessness being out of control in cities and the violence killing small businesses and the police." Among black, Hispanic, and Asian voters, worries about crime and homelessness topped the list.

Emphasizing these issues could particularly help the Republicans gain more non-white working-class voters, among whom they have already been improving. However, Republicans too often seem unconcerned about remedying ongoing problems with policing and the criminal justice system, which can make their party's stance on these issues appear one-dimensional—concerned only with tougher enforcement. In our survey, we asked whether police officers treat blacks and other minorities the same as whites. Overall, 59 percent of respondents said no, including 68 percent of working-class non-whites. The Republicans' challenge will therefore be to embody a tough but fair approach. This could allow them to considerably broaden their coalition by consolidating more non-white working-class voters.

Identity Politics. In recent years, huge swaths of the Democratic Party have become enamored of an ideology that judges actions or arguments not by their content but rather by the identity of those engaging in them. Those identities in turn are defined by an intersectional web of oppressors or oppressed, powerful or powerless, and dominant or marginalized. With this approach, one does not judge actions by their effectiveness or arguments by their truth; rather, one judges them by whether the people doing or making them are in the oppressed, powerless, or marginalized group. If so, the actions or arguments deserve support; if not, they deserve opposition.

This approach obviously contradicts logic and common sense. And it has led much of the Democratic Party to take positions that have little purchase in social or political reality and offend most Americans' basic values.

Nothing exemplifies this better than the lionization of Ibram X. Kendi, whose thoroughly ridiculous claims somehow came to be treated as revealed truth by some on the left. Kendi wrote, for instance:

There is no such thing as a nonracist or race-neutral policy. Every policy in every institution in every community in every nation is producing or sustaining either racial inequity or equity, racial justice or injustice. . . .

The only remedy to negative racist discrimination that produces inequity is positive antiracist discrimination that produces equity. The only remedy to past negative racist discrimination that has produced inequity is present positive antiracist discrimination that produces equity. The only remedy to present negative racist discrimination toward inequity is future positive antiracist discrimination toward equity.

Only those who have checked their capacity for critical thinking at the door could possibly take such arguments seriously. But many Democrats did.

Most voters recognize bad ideas and arguments. It doesn't matter who makes them. Voters generally embrace instead the universal principles embedded in traditional American values. They believe, unlike Kendi, that racial preferences in rewards and decision-making are not fair, and fairness is fundamental to their worldview. They believe, with Martin Luther King Jr., that people should "not be judged by the color of their skin but by the content of their character."

A 2022 University of Southern California Dornsife survey posed this classic statement of color-blind equality: "Our goal as a society should be to treat all people the same without regard to the color of their skin." That view elicited sky-high (92 percent) agreement from the public, despite the assaults on it from sectors of the left.

Similarly, a 2023 survey by Public Agenda's Hidden Common Ground Initiative found 91 percent of respondents agreed that "all people deserve an equal opportunity to succeed, no matter their race or ethnicity." People deeply believe in equal opportunity, not equal outcomes, contrary to the intersectional ideology so thoroughly entrenched in the Democratic Party.

Obviously, this presents a tremendous opportunity for Republicans. They can occupy the high ground of equal opportunity, reward for merit, and common sense. This potentially allows them to appeal to the great majority of working-class voters, as our survey's findings indicate.

Take structural racism. Is racism "built into our society, including into its policies and institutions," per Democratic Party orthodoxy, or does it "come from individuals who hold racist views, not from our society and institutions"? In our survey, moderate-to-conservative, non-white, working-class voters (70 percent of whom are moderate, not conservative) were more likely to choose the latter view—that racism comes from individuals, not society (61 percent vs. 39 percent). Hispanic voters as a whole essentially agreed. In stark contrast, the comparatively tiny population of non-white, liberal college graduates were more likely to favor the structural racism explanation (78 percent vs. 20 percent). White, liberal college graduates were even more lopsided in favoring this explanation (82 percent vs. 18 percent). That tells you a lot about who influences the Democratic Party today on racial issues.

Note that this is not because voters believe there is no racism or discrimination in American society. Our survey found that even white working-class voters are more likely than not to believe blacks face significant discrimination today, and non-white working-class voters believe this overwhelmingly. The same goes for whether increasing racial and ethnic diversity is good for American society. Even white working-class voters are far more likely than not to think this trend is good for America, and non-white working-class voters, unsurprisingly, overwhelmingly approve of this change.

In a survey whose results support our findings about Americans' decidedly non-radical social views, RMG Research tested statements about these views among 18,000 registered voters, asking them to agree or disagree. The statements included:

Equality of opportunity is a fundamental American principle; equality of outcome is not. (Seventy-three percent of respondents agreed, while 13 percent disagreed.)

Discrimination and racism are bad, but they are not the cause of all disparities in American society. (Seventy percent of respondents agreed, while 24 percent disagreed.)

No one is completely without bias, but calling all white people racists who benefit from white privilege and calling America a white supremacist society is not right or fair. (Seventy-seven percent of respondents agreed, while 15 percent disagreed.)

This is far from the equity- and identity-oriented radicalism closely associated with the Democrats. No one should have been surprised at the strong public approval (roughly two-thirds, including a majority of blacks) for the Supreme Court's decision to strike down affirmative action and racial preferences in college admissions.

Racial preferences are simply unpopular and have been for a long time. In a typical pre-decision result, SCOTUSPoll researchers from Harvard University, Stanford University, and the University of Texas found 69 percent of the public agreed that private colleges and universities should not be allowed to consider race in admissions, compared with 31 percent who thought they should. When the researchers posed the same question about public colleges and universities, it produced a 74 percent versus 26 percent split along the same lines.

A Pew Research Center poll in 2022 found just 7 percent of the public thought high school grades should not factor into college admissions, and a mere 14 percent thought standardized test scores should not. But an overwhelming 74 percent thought race or ethnicity should not factor into college admissions.

This pattern held among all non-white racial groups. Among blacks, 59 percent said race should not factor into college admissions, compared with 11 percent who said high school grades should not be a factor and 21 percent who said this about standardized tests. Hispanics (68 percent) and Asians (63 percent) were even more likely to oppose the consideration of race in admissions. In short, equal opportunity and antidiscrimination are popular. Identity politics is not.

Another example of the Democrats' cultural radicalism involves transgender issues—that is, the policy and medical issues around those who identify as a different gender from their biological sex. Many Democrats' approach to these issues has gone far beyond advocating antidiscrimination—in the manner of the gay rights movement—to embracing the ideology that men identifying as female are exactly the same as biological women and must have unrestricted access to all women's sports teams, bathrooms, changing rooms, rape and domestic abuse shelters, prisons, and so forth. Perhaps most controversially, minors who claim to be a gender different from their biological sex must be "affirmed" and provided medical treatments like puberty blockers, hormones, and surgery to align their bodies as much as possible with their "gender identity," despite the increasingly strong evidence that such treatments' benefits to patients do not outweigh their considerable costs.

All this is unpopular with voters, especially working-class voters, who buy neither the ideology nor the practices that Democrats have enthusiastically promulgated. In our survey, we asked: Should "transgender athletes . . . be able to play on sports teams that match their current gender identity," or should they "only be allowed to play on sports teams that match their birth gender?" Overwhelmingly, voters chose the second option—that birth gender should determine sports team participation—over the first (70 percent vs. 26 percent).

Working-class voters across races held this opinion; overall, they were far more likely to oppose than favor allowing sports participation based on gender identity (74 percent vs. 23 percent), and both white and non-white working-class voters were against the practice (80 percent vs. 18 percent and 65 percent vs. 30 percent, respectively). Indeed, across demographics, there was little support for sports participation based on gender identity. The exception: Non-white and white liberal college graduates were each more likely to endorse the Democrats' stance on gender identity by a 40 percentage point margin. Here again, Democrats' most educated and liberal supporters are driving the issue.

The Republican Opportunity on Cultural Radicalism. These data's message could not be clearer: On a wide range of cultural issues, Democrats are in step with their most educated, liberal supporters but emphatically out of step with the median voter, particularly the median working-class voter. This obviously provides Republicans with a tremendous opportunity to expand their coalition by standing for a moderate, commonsense approach to these issues, including by defending the core ideal of antidiscrimination, which the Democrats are now shying away from.

However, that is not the same as favoring an enforcement-only approach to crime and immigration, denying the continued existence of racism, and adopting extreme "anti-woke" rhetoric. Voters don't like the Democrats' position on these issues, but they are uncomfortable with the most aggressive arguments for the other side as well.

Energy Realism. Since the days of Obama's moderate all-of-the-above approach to energy production, the Democrats have embraced a radical stance on energy issues. It is fair to say the bulk of Democrats more or less believe that climate change is not a gradually advancing dynamic but an imminent crisis that is already upon us, that it is evident in extreme weather events, and that it threatens the planet's existence if immediate, drastic action is not taken. That action must include immediately replacing fossil fuels, including natural gas, with renewables—wind and solar. These renewable energy forms are cheap, can be introduced now if sufficient resources are devoted to doing so, and (unlike nuclear power) are safe. Furthermore, immediately replacing fossil fuels with renewables will make energy cheaper and provide high-wage jobs.

In this dominant Democratic view, people resist rapidly eliminating fossil fuels only because of the fossil fuel industry's propaganda. Any problems with renewables, such as their intermittency and unreliability, are being solved. This means that as we use more renewables and cut fossil fuels, political support for the transition to clean energy should increase because of the benefits to consumers and workers.

Pretty much every sentence in this catechism is highly and substantively debatable. But it cannot be questioned among Democrats. A 2023 New York Times article detailed Biden's aggressive climate plans—which Harris has not disavowed—for his second term, featuring heavy regulation of steel and cement plants, factories, and oil refineries. That seems unlikely to endear the Democrats to workers in those industries.

The same article quotes Biden as asserting, "The only existential threat humanity faces even more frightening than a nuclear war is global warming going above 1.5 degrees in the next 20—10 years. That'd be real trouble. There's no way back from that."

The implication is that we can't worry about trivial things like workers' jobs in high-paying industries, consumer prices for energy, or even the technical feasibility of maximalist energy-transition plans. Hence the Democrats' commitment to the fanciful goal of attaining net-zero emissions by 2050 to limit global warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius. Hence their commitment to extremely rapid elimination of fossil fuel use. Hence their commitment to an equally rapid buildup of wind and solar energy production.

This is all gospel to the Democrats' most educated, liberal supporters, who consider themselves noble warriors against the impending apocalypse. But most voters, especially working-class voters, have not signed up for—and are not even interested in—the rapid green transition that the party envisions. Therefore, when the push toward net zero threatens their jobs, living standards, or lifestyles, they are unlikely to cut the Democrats much slack. As the Times article just cited noted:

While 54 percent of adults polled by Pew said climate change was a major threat to the country's well-being, respondents ranked it 17th out of 21 national issues in a January survey. "Even for Democrats, who say it's important, it's not the top issue," said Alec Tyson, a researcher who helped conduct the survey.

Workers prefer a gradual, all-of-the-above approach to transitioning the energy system over the frantic push for renewables and electric vehicles (not to mention heat pumps and electric stoves) that characterizes Democrats' Green New Deal–type thinking. In a YouGov survey in 2023, just a quarter of working-class (noncollege-educated) voters embraced the Democrats' approach emphasizing ending fossil fuel use and rapidly adopting renewables. This was less than the share (29 percent) who flat-out supported fossil fuel production and opposed green energy projects. The dominant position by far was an all-of-the-above approach that called for cheap, abundant energy from many sources, including oil, gas, renewables, and nuclear power, which 46 percent of voters favored.

Our survey's findings were consistent with these attitudes. We asked respondents whether they thought the country's energy supply should "use a mix of energy sources including oil, coal and natural gas along with renewable energy sources" or "phase out the use of oil, coal and natural gas completely, relying instead on renewable energy sources such as wind and solar power only." An overwhelming 72 percent favored the all-of-the-above approach, including fossil fuels, while just 26 percent backed the rapid transition to renewables.

The split was even more lopsided among working-class respondents than among political moderates. Predictably, liberal, white college graduates were an exception—two to one in favor of eliminating fossil fuels. But moderate and conservative white college graduates, who vastly outnumber liberals, were almost seven to one against the policy. Since Democrats increasingly rely on white college graduates' support, this indicates the breadth of Democrats' vulnerability on energy issues. Even more startlingly, our survey found that among voters who planned to support the Democrats in the coming election and voters who supported Biden in 2020, solid majorities favored the all-of-the-above approach and opposed replacing fossil fuels with renewables.

Consumer price issues will likely be crucial in the politics of any energy transition. How much are voters willing to pay to facilitate the fight against climate change? Our survey investigated this and found that the short answer is not much.

We asked respondents whether they would vote for a ballot measure to pay extra on their monthly utility bill to combat climate change, with proposed amounts ranging from $1 to $100. Even for $1, the public was closely split, with respondents' willingness to support that fee just 7 points higher than their opposition to it. Most working-class respondents weren't even willing to go that far, in contrast to the college educated, whose support for the $1 fee was 20 points higher. This 20-point differential held for members of the working class versus college graduates, both white and non-white. Notably, political independents were two to one against paying the $1 extra.

When we upped the ante to $10 a month, things really started to fall apart. By a 30-point margin, working-class respondents were more likely to say they would vote against than for the measure. Among moderates and independents, that margin was 20 points.

Raising the proposed levy to combat climate change to $20 a month appeared to reach a breaking point. Not only did working-class respondents say they would vote against it by a 40-point margin, but even college graduates bailed out, opposing the extra fee by a 20-point margin. Moderates were opposed by a 40-point margin and independents by a 50-point margin. Even voters who planned to support the Democrats in 2024 and those who voted for Biden in 2020 moved into the opposition at this point.

Above $20, the levies garnered increasingly tiny support. In fact, even liberal white college graduates couldn't bring themselves to vote in favor of a $75 levy. Apparently, those most sure that climate change is an existential crisis have a fairly low ceiling on what they're willing to pay to avoid the impending apocalypse.

These data indicate weak buy-in among ordinary voters for the Democrats' crusade to transition rapidly to renewables and electric vehicles and eliminate fossil fuels. To the extent Democrats insist on continuing down this road—and so far they give no indication of abandoning their crusade, despite Harris's frantic backpedaling on issues like fracking—energy policy is an electoral Achilles' heel for them and a significant opportunity for Republicans. Commonsense energy realism should easily outcompete Democrats' obsessive drive for a rapid, renewables-based energy transition. That could allow the GOP to expand its coalition among working-class voters and moderate college graduates.

However, Republicans must recognize that voters still are sympathetic to expanding renewables in the energy system, even if not in the Democrats' single-minded manner. Our poll found that between "developing alternative sources such as wind, solar and hydrogen technology" and "expanding exploration and production of oil, coal and natural gas," 38 percent of respondents favored the former and just 15 percent the latter, and 47 percent thought both approaches should have equal priority. Republicans must ensure they don't come off as simply against renewables.

The same goes for dismissing climate change as a problem. In our survey, 65 percent of voters thought climate change is a very or moderately big problem in the country today, including 63 percent of working-class voters. Republicans cannot afford to seem indifferent to it, thereby opening themselves to the "climate denier" charge, which Democrats will be only too happy to lodge.

Patriotism. The Democratic Party suffers from a patriotism gap. It is viewed as the less patriotic party, and Democrats are less likely than Republicans and independents to consider themselves patriotic. Poll after poll has found this to be true.

A poll that Third Way and Impact Research conducted in late 2022 found that 56 percent of voters characterized the Republican Party as "patriotic," compared with 46 percent who thought the same about the Democrats.

In two 3,000-voter surveys that YouGov conducted in June and September 2023, only 29 percent of voters thought the Democrats were closer than the Republicans to their own views on patriotism, while 43 percent thought the reverse. Exactly twice as many working-class (noncollege-educated) voters thought the Republicans were closer to their own views on patriotism as thought the Democrats were closer (48 percent vs. 24 percent). Interestingly, college-educated voters differed little in how close they felt to each party on patriotism.

In a poll of 2,500 voters in battleground states and districts in November 2023, PSG Consulting and Greenberg Research found Trump and the Republicans had an 11-point advantage over Biden and the Democrats in respondents' opinions of who would do a better job on "being patriotic."

In Gallup's latest reading on pride in being American, 55 percent of Democrats said they were extremely or very proud of being American, compared with 64 percent of independents and 85 percent of Republicans. Just 29 percent of Democrats characterized themselves as "extremely proud," down 25 points since the beginning of this century.

Perhaps most strikingly, in a 2022 poll, Quinnipiac University found that a majority of Democrats (52 percent) said they would leave the country, rather than stay and fight (40 percent), should the United States be invaded as Ukraine was by Russia.

Data from our survey are completely consistent with these findings. Almost two-thirds of voters (64 percent) viewed the Republicans as patriotic, compared with under half (49 percent) who thought the Democrats were. The split was substantially larger among working-class voters (67 percent to 45 percent).

We also asked whether voters considered America the greatest country in the world. Most white, liberal college graduates were sure America is not the greatest country in the world: 73 percent endorsed that view. But the multiracial working class thought the reverse: 62 percent said America is the greatest country in the world.

So the patriotism gap is alive, well, and persistent. Why? One key factor is that, for much of the Democrats' progressive base, being patriotic is uncool and hard to square with its political outlook. As Brink Lindsey put it in an essay titled "The Loss of Faith":

The most flamboyantly anti-American rhetoric of 60s radicals is now more or less conventional wisdom among many progressives: America, the land of white supremacy and structural racism and patriarchy, the perpetrator of indigenous displacement and genocide, the world's biggest polluter, and so on. There are patriotic counter-currents on the center-left—think of Obama's speech at the 2004 Democratic convention, or Hamilton—but these days both feel awfully dated.

That "conventional wisdom" is a problem. It's why "Progressive Activists"—who comprise 8 percent of the population, according to More in Common, and are "deeply concerned with issues concerning equity, fairness, and America's direction today"—are so unenthusiastic about their country. Just 34 percent of these activists say they are "proud to be American," compared with 62 percent of Asians, 70 percent of blacks, and 76 percent of Hispanics, the very groups whose interests they claim to represent.

In fact, these sentiments, and the view of America they represent, are heavily associated with Democrats by dint of progressive activists' significant weight in the party, which far transcends their actual numbers. Their voice is amplified by their strong and frequently dominant influence in associated institutions that lean Democratic: nonprofits, foundations, advocacy groups, academia, legacy media, and the arts—the commanding heights of cultural production, as it were. It's just not cool in these circles to be patriotic.

Why does this matter? Obviously, it puts the Democrats on the wrong side of something that's quite popular: patriotism and love of country. Indeed, this has become so obvious that Democrats at their national convention tried fairly ostentatiously to emphasize their patriotic bona fides. But realistically, changing public perceptions of their party will take the Democrats considerable time, since voters formed those perceptions over numerous years.

Even after a decade of decline in our contentious times, 67 percent of the public say they are extremely or very proud of being an American. Another 22 percent say they are moderately proud. And, as liberal commentator Noah Smith correctly observes: "People want to like their country. They can be disappointed in it or mad at it or frustrated with it, but ultimately they want to think that they're part of something good." (Emphasis in original.) Making people feel bad about their country is a significant political liability for Democrats—and correspondingly a great opportunity for Republicans, who are already viewed as the more patriotic party by a wide margin.

### Democratic Opportunities

The Democratic coalition has become increasingly dependent on the votes of college graduates, particularly white college graduates. Its long-standing, worsening weakness is among white working-class voters, still a vast constituency despite their steadily declining weight in the electorate. Of course, the burgeoning non-white population—especially blacks but also Hispanics and Asians—continues to vote heavily Democratic. But the Democratic margins among these groups are starting to decline, especially among Hispanics and working-class voters.

Geographically, Democrats' support base is extremely polarized. Democrats dominate urban areas and run up ever-bigger margins in inner suburbs, but their strength diminishes away from the urban core. And in much of rural and small-town America, their brand is simply toxic.

The Democrats' coalition mirrors the Republicans' in strength, enough to stalemate the GOP but not enough to dominate. This is not for lack of opportunities to expand their coalition but rather from an unwillingness to remedy political vulnerabilities (as outlined in "Republican Opportunities") and leverage their key opportunities to the maximum extent.

These are their biggest opportunities.

Abortion. At this point, Democrats unquestionably have a significant advantage in the abortion debate that has been taking shape since the Supreme Court overturned Roe. This relates to the perception that Republicans are eager to outlaw access to abortion wholesale, while the precise nature and boundaries of the Democrats' preferred approach are not well-defined in the public mind. But the Democratic advantage is also a function of long-standing trends. For example, Pew Research Center data show that Americans' support for legalizing abortion has steadily risen over time. Support was at a low point in 2009, when just 47 percent of respondents said abortion should be legal in all or most cases and 44 percent said it should be illegal. Now, 63 percent say abortion should be completely or mostly legal, and 36 percent say the opposite.

Similarly, Gallup data show the percentage of Americans saying abortion should be legal in all circumstances—though still a minority—is at its highest ever. And Gallup finds the percentage of Americans saying they're pro-choice (54 percent) as opposed to pro-life (41 percent) is at its highest in the 21st century. Moreover, these data indicate that the issue's political salience has increased, especially since Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization, and is higher among pro-choice than pro-life voters.

Political developments since Dobbs indicate that Democrats are well positioned on the abortion issue. For example, in August 2023, voters in conservative Kansas defeated by 18 percentage points an attempt to amend the state constitution to specify that there was no constitutional right to an abortion.

Similarly, in bright-red Ohio, the electorate voted twice in favor of abortion rights. First, in August 2023, voters defeated by 14 points a referendum designed to make enshrining abortion rights in the state constitution harder. Shortly thereafter, a ballot initiative to do just that passed easily by 13 points. And earlier that year, in Wisconsin, liberal Janet Protasiewicz trounced conservative Daniel Kelly for a state supreme court seat by 10 points in an election fought largely on abortion.

There are many other examples, but the pattern is clear. Voters even in red and purple states generally support abortion access, which makes their position closer to what they understand as the view of the Democrats rather than the Republicans, whom they still consider militantly pro-life, including supporting abortion bans.

Our survey's results support this assessment. We asked the same question Pew has traditionally asked and found a similar overall split between voters who thought abortion should be always or mostly legal versus always or mostly illegal (64 percent vs. 34 percent). Not surprisingly, 70 percent of college graduates supported the always-or-mostly-legal position, but so did 67 percent of the non-white working class and 57 percent of the white working class. Within the white working class, most moderates (65 percent) held this position.

This suggests the abortion issue could help Democrats expand their coalition among the white working class and shore up their deteriorating position among the non-white working class. But there are warning signs for Democrats here as well.

First, election after election has shown that support for Democrats runs far behind support for legal abortion in state referenda. In other words, a large number of voters appear comfortable voting for generally pro-life Republicans even while they hold pro-choice views for which they would vote in a single-issue referendum. Thus, while the issue cross-pressures Republican-leaning voters and demographics, it is by no means automatically realigning.

Second, Democrats do best on this issue and will continue to do best when they can frame it as a question of general abortion access versus a ban on the procedure. But allowing unrestricted access to abortion throughout a pregnancy is not popular. Most voters want some limits on the procedure, which conflicts with the views of leaders of the Democrats' highly influential abortion rights wing, who oppose any limits. The public tends to think Democrats are more moderate on abortion than they actually are at this point, and Republicans have an obvious interest in changing that and helping voters see where Democratic politicians really stand.

Our survey tested the limits issue with a question Gallup has traditionally asked about support for abortion in each trimester of pregnancy. We found 71 percent of respondents supported legal abortion during the first trimester (94 percent of abortions occur in the first 13 weeks of pregnancy), 39 percent during the second trimester, and just 20 percent during the third trimester. Legalizing the procedure during the second trimester received support from 35 percent of working-class non-whites and 31 percent of working-class whites. For abortion during the third trimester, support dropped to 19 percent among working-class non-whites and 14 percent among working-class whites. Indeed, just 27 percent of white college graduates believe abortion should be generally legal during the third trimester (which is the current view of most if not all elected Democrats at the national level), and 72 percent believe the reverse.

Seemingly, only a moderate stance on legal abortion—that is, the willingness to accept some restrictions and limits on it—would allow Democrats to maximize their gains on this issue and expand their coalition.

Health Care. Another clear opportunity for the Democrats involves access to affordable health care. Democrats are consistently the party voters trust to handle health care. That trust extends to various health care issues. Kaiser Family Foundation (KFF) polling earlier in the 2024 campaign found that respondents trusted Biden over Trump by a 12-point margin on determining the ACA's future, by a 12-point margin on protecting people with preexisting medical conditions, by an 11-point margin on ensuring access to affordable health insurance, and by an 8-point margin on determining Medicare's future.

After a famously rocky and unpopular start, Obama's ACA has steadily gained favor. The split of favorable versus unfavorable views on the Democratic-identified health care reform bill is now 62 percent to 37 percent in the KFF polling cited above.

On the broad question of the government's responsibilities for health care, sentiment has shifted back to a strongly pro-government position after dipping during Obama's second term. According to Gallup, 59 percent of voters now say the government is responsible for ensuring all people have health care coverage, compared with 40 percent who disagree. This sentiment obviously favors the Democrats.

Health care is important to American voters. In our survey, 88 percent of voters thought health care affordability is a very or moderately big problem in the country today. That represents the largest consensus on any issue we tested, except inflation. And the sentiment was uniform across most demographics, including the white and non-white working class.

In a 2024 YouGov survey that tested support or opposition to a wide variety of proposals associated with the Democratic or Republican Party, the top-performing proposals—which performed remarkably well—were almost all health care related. In order of net support (support minus opposition), the health care proposals were:

"Increase the number of prescription drugs that Medicare can negotiate the price of for seniors." (Net support was 75 points.)

"Require pharmaceutical companies to charge American consumers the lowest price they charge consumers in foreign countries." (Net support was 75 points.)

"Cap the cost of insulin at $35 a month for every American who needs it." (Net support was 74 points.)

"Protect Medicare and Social Security from funding cuts or increases in the age of eligibility." (Net support was 72 points.)

"Permanently extend the Affordable Care Act (ACA) protections for those with pre-existing conditions." (Net support was 59 points.)

"Cap prescription drug costs at $2,000 per year for all Americans." (Net support was 51 points.)

Support for these policies is strong across demographics but tends to be stronger among working-class voters, particularly white working-class voters. Democrats' opportunity to expand their coalition through this issue is clear. Remember that in the 2018 elections, when the Democrats performed so well, health care issues were absolutely central to congressional campaigns.

But Democrats face challenges on this front too. One is that elevating health care comes with an opportunity cost that Democrats, with their fervent commitments to "democracy is on the ballot," abortion rights, and climate change, may not be willing to pay. More attention to health care means less attention to these other causes. The Democrats' educated, liberal base and infrastructure may resist that—even if a net enlargement of their coalition would result.

Consider the Inflation Reduction Act, which included significant health care–related spending on insurance subsidies and instituted Medicare price bargaining and other cost-control measures for some prescription drugs. The act was publicized and understood as involving climate change, renewable energy, and electric vehicles, which completely overshadowed its health care provisions. To this day, the overwhelming majority of voters are not even aware of these provisions, according to KFF polling.

That explains why Biden's lead over Trump in whom voters trusted to address high health care costs was smaller than his lead in other health care areas. Given the centrality of cost to voters' health care concerns, Democrats cannot fully capitalize on their overall health care advantage without overcoming this weakness.

Political Norms. Democrats have placed a big bet on the political efficacy of "democracy is on the ballot" and similar messages about how democracy is in peril and can be preserved only by voting for Democrats. It is actually not at all clear that Democrats have an advantage on the issue of "democracy" or that this rhetoric is a good way to expand their coalition. But they do have an advantage on political norms—that is, conducting politics in the moderate, trustworthy ways most voters want. Our survey demonstrates this.

To begin with, by far the main reason so many voters are pessimistic about the country's future is that they sense the political system is "broken." No other reason comes even close. This sentiment is shared across demographics, including among white and non-white working-class voters.

Our survey compared how well respondents thought various phrases applied to the Democratic and Republican Parties. While Republicans performed notably better than Democrats on "values hard work" and "patriotic," Democrats were preferred on "respectful and tolerant of different types of people" (with a 14-point advantage) and "respectful and tolerant of their political opponents" (with a 10-point advantage). This Democratic advantage held across working-class voters.

The Republicans' close association with Trump accentuates the Democrats' advantage in political norms. According to multiple polls, voters find Trump's character and honesty deficient and disapprove of his election denialism and connection to the riot at the Capitol on January 6, 2021. They also believe at least some of the many charges against him are valid. These opinions extend among many voters in Republicans' most favorable demographics, whose support the GOP relies on.

Democrats, therefore, can expand their coalition if they focus on defending core political norms—regarding which most voters are on their side—rather than apocalyptic claims about the imminent demise of democracy (or "freedom"), about which voters are far more skeptical and divided. However, the Democrats' activist core of liberal college graduates may make this reorientation difficult, despite the potential political payoff, since they appear less interested in fixing broken politics than pillorying the other side as stalking horses for fascism.

### Jump Balls

Two big areas offer opportunities for both parties and therefore, however they currently lean, are "jump balls" between the parties.

Economic Prosperity. One jump ball is the case for an abundant, prosperous economy that could make voters forget the 21st century's relative economic stagnation. Most American voters, especially those in the working class, are pessimistic about the country's future, according to our survey. And they are open to hearing a case for doing better from either party.

Right now, Republicans are favored on the economy generally, but this is heavily driven by Democrats' identification with Biden-era inflation. It is far less clear that voters have confidence in, or even understand, the GOP's economic plans for the country. While working-class voters have received the Trump campaign's populist rhetoric well, the economic policy behind that rhetoric is a confusing mix of hawkishness on trade, commitment to entitlement programs, tax cuts, immigration restrictions, and manufacturing-oriented industrial policy. This may add up to a successful new economic model, but there are considerable grounds for skepticism. And if it doesn't produce the outcomes voters—particularly working-class voters—desire, they will be quick to turn on it.

As for the Democrats, their bid to institute a new economic strategy for the country has so far failed to produce anything resembling economic abundance. Their "investments" in this new strategy focus heavily on a clean energy transition and have failed to address the various regulatory and permitting bottlenecks that inhibit rapid growth. And of course, inflation has proved to be far more of a problem than they had anticipated. The upshot is that Democrats have not reestablished voters' confidence in their economic management skills, to say the least. It seems doubtful that the Harris campaign's turn toward economic populism will do much to restore that confidence; it is hard to persuade voters to disregard their experience.

That's a far cry from what the Democrats once enjoyed. For 73 years, Gallup has asked which party can "do a better job keeping the country prosperous" in the next several years. In the first part of this period, from 1951 to Reagan's election, Democrats had a large, robust advantage on this measure, averaging a 17-point lead over the Republicans. But from Reagan's election on, that advantage vanished. While there have been many ups and downs, Republicans have averaged a slight advantage (2 points) in which party voters believe can keep the country prosperous. One way to think about this is that Democrats are no longer the default party for American prosperity in voters' eyes, but they have not been decisively replaced either.

America's Global Position. The other big area up for grabs is America's place in the world. Again, voters are unhappy with the currently unsettled nature of the world, which has advantaged the GOP relative to the Democrats regarding which party voters think can better handle foreign policy. But voters' fundamental attitudes toward America's place in the world are fear and dissatisfaction.

That fear is particularly focused on China. Unfavorable views of China have skyrocketed, as have fears of its emerging military power. And China is now seen, by a wide margin, as America's greatest enemy in the world today.

That fear and an increasingly chaotic global situation have led to widespread dissatisfaction with America's position in the world. Since the early 2000s, satisfaction with America's global position has plummeted, breaking 50 percent only once and now standing at just 33 percent. Perceptions of national decline are common, and overwhelming majorities think the US is becoming less important relative to other countries. In the 21st century, neither party has managed to restore American voters' confidence in their country's role in the world.

A System in Deadlock. That these two broad, highly significant issues—economic policy and national defense, which are essentially the two concerns our political system assigns most forthrightly to the national government—are jump balls is a sign of this political moment's peculiar deadlock and the American party system's ongoing failure. Voters are divided on these issues not because they are starkly polarized around two clear visions from the parties but because they are unpersuaded that either party offers a coherent vision on either issue.

## XI. The Next Majority

This overview of the history of American party politics, today's two major party coalitions, and each party's opportunities and challenges has put in stark relief the party system's broader failure. For a generation, Americans have been in a state of deadlock, with neither party able to attract a durable majority of voters or construct a coherent winning coalition.

This deadlock has not happened because either party lacks opportunities. Nor has it resulted because the party coalitions have been static and unchanging. Today's Democratic and Republican voters are not yesterday's. Both party coalitions have churned a great deal, yet neither has broken through to clear majority status.

Surveying the parties' decisions in one election cycle after another, it is hard to avoid concluding that they are stuck at 50–50 because they choose to be. Both have prioritized the wishes of their most intensely devoted voters—who would never vote for the other party—over the priorities of winnable voters who could go either way. They have done this even as the nature of their most devoted voters has changed. They have not operated as institutions geared to construct broad coalitions and win broad general-election victories. Instead, they have focused on fan service—satisfying their most partisan and loyal constituencies.

Ironically, the fact of America's 50–50 politics has made it difficult for either party to break out of this pattern. You might think that two minority parties would each feel pressure to expand its coalition and become a majority, but actually, both have behaved as if they were the rightful majorities already. Each finds ways to dismiss the other's wins as narrow flukes and treat its own as massive triumphs. Indeed, each has responded to close election losses with various forms of denial.

This is sustainable only because recent elections have been so close. Politicians learn big lessons from big losses or big wins, so neither party has learned much in a long time, and neither can grasp that it isn't popular and could easily lose the next election.

Breaking this pattern must start by acknowledging a truism: Bigger majorities are possible if the parties seek broader support. That sounds obvious, yet it has eluded America's leaders for a generation, because it requires seeing beyond our age of deadlock. But it remains true.

Deadlock is not the American party system's natural equilibrium. Durable majority coalitions are not only possible; they are the norm. And we will see them again. But the next durable majority must result from self-conscious coalition building, which in turn must result from realizing that what both parties are now doing is not working and will not work.

Whichever party first grasps that it has been losing for a generation will have a chance to make itself the next big winner in American politics.
