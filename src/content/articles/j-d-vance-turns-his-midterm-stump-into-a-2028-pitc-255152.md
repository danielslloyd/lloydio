# J.D. Vance Turns His Midterm Stump Into a 2028 Pitch

*On the campaign trail, the vice president is boosting his boss’s agenda while refining his own vision.*

Greetings from Michigan, where yesterday I watched Vice President J.D. Vance rally Republican voters for the second time this week. On Monday, Vance and I were both in Montana. Yes, you read that correctly: a rally in deep-red Montana the week before Election Day.

My mini-review of Montana as a first-time visitor: It earns the moniker “Big Sky Country.” It was a thrill to drive over the Continental Divide, even if it meant white-knuckling it through some very thick fog on a mountain. Downtown Missoula is charming, even if I nearly hit a deer with my rental car one morning in the middle of a busy neighborhood. I’d love to go back to the great state of Montana sometime, but I hope it’s in the summer—the mountain chill of late October isn’t my thing.

In this T-minus 6 issue, I take a look at Vance’s closing arguments from the trail. Also, we’re in the Halloween spirit here at *Next Right*, so stay until the end for some recent sightings of Republican zombies.

*Vice President J.D. Vance speaks at a midterm campaign rally at CHI Aviation on October 27, 2026, in Howell, Michigan. (Photo by Saul Loeb / Pool / AFP via Getty Images)*

There’s no denying that J.D. Vance, Donald Trump’s vice president and heir apparent to the MAGA movement, has been dealt a bad hand in these final days of the midterm campaign. The country’s in an anti-incumbent mood. Trump has perhaps worn out his welcome with voters after 10 years. Prices on necessities are stubbornly high.

During a Monday rally in Montana, where the average cost of diesel is well over $6 a gallon, the veep was reduced to reminding his audience that, hey, petroleum prices have been high before, and it’s not the Trump administration’s fault.

“There’s still so much work to do on correcting the terrible Biden inflation, the highest inflation that we saw in 50 years in our country,” Vance said at the event in Belgrade, a town near Bozeman, where I had just paid $4.17 a gallon for gasoline. “But … remember, in 2022, when Russia invaded Ukraine and gas prices skyrocketed? They skyrocketed, by the way, far higher than they are today. They skyrocketed in 1979. They skyrocketed in 2001. They skyrocketed in 2007.”

Fair enough, except this particular surge in oil prices can be blamed directly on a military operation and protracted conflict that Vance’s boss launched unilaterally, without much warning, and with no buy-in from Congress or the public. “They skyrocket every time some tinpot dictator decides to throw a tantrum,” Vance went on, perhaps not quite grasping how this line might land with critics of the president. (The next day in Michigan, he tweaked the line, referring more specifically to “foreign leaders in the Middle East.”)

But voters understand, and they’re itching to send a message to the president whom they blame for the high cost of gasoline, groceries, and other necessities that he was elected to fix, not exacerbate. It’s a big, glaring reason GOP candidates in places as red as Montana (and Kansas and Iowa and on and on) are struggling to keep swing voters on side or at the very least excite their base. The latter problem explains why Trump and Vance are running through the tape with rally after rally.

Trump’s final midterm push has more to do with his own vanity, but the 42-year-old vice president has something more substantial at stake. If Republicans are losing “should have won” races in Montana or Kansas, it could be seen as a rebuke not just of Trump but of the whole presidency that Vance has taken to calling (not unlike his predecessor Mike Pence did) the “Trump-Vance administration.” So with 2028 on the horizon, Vance is taking the current opportunity to—carefully, respectfully, with all outward deference toward Trump—stake his own claim to represent the future of the GOP and the country.

While stumping for Republican candidates, Vance remains a steadfast defender of the rosiest version of the president’s record: eliminating taxes on tips, securing the southern border, lowering crime rates. But he is also using these final days of the campaign to workshop the pitch for J.D. Vance, arguing that he will be a purer and more focused practitioner of the new Republican populism.

Instead of pledging to build upon the overflowing successes of Trump’s two nonconsecutive terms, Vance reaches back to the pre-Trump political status quo, painting a picture of a bipartisan, globalist establishment that sucked Middle America dry to serve its investments and wars overseas. In 2016, Trump successfully ran in a Republican primary against the presidency and legacy of George W. Bush, and it seems Vance wants to return to that well.

“I remember distinctly wondering why it was that politicians seemed to care so little for the places that farmed our food, that built our cars, and that sent our troops to fight in war,” Vance said yesterday at a rally in Howell, Michigan, about an hour outside of Detroit. “Be honest, ladies and gentlemen. You know this as well as I do. It was not just Democrats. It was everybody.”

What Vance says “our movement” is really about is restoring “sovereignty” to the American people. This particular word seems to be a favorite of his; I heard him utter some form of it 10 times at his Montana rally and nine times in Michigan. Here’s a sampling:

> “Sovereignty starts when you control more of your own money.”
>
> “Sovereignty means that you control your streets.”
>
> “Sovereignty means that American homes and American farmland ought, by right, to belong to the American people.”
>
> “Sovereignty means defending our country and our children’s future from those who seek to destroy it. It’s defending our country from those who seek to tear down our culture, our way of life, and the common bonds which unite us.”
>
> “Sovereignty is when Donald J. Trump called those pharma companies into the Oval Office and demanded they treat Americans fairly and lower your drug prices.”

Vance seems to use “sovereignty” to mean anything good and popular. But I couldn’t help but notice some of the other words Vance uses around this concept: “control,” “ought,” and “demand.”

Yes, these are just words, but I think they are as good an indication as any that the thoroughly unphilosophical Trump has refocused the political philosophy of the conservative movement away from “free to choose” and toward government authority and action. Vance, looking to seize the MAGA mantle, has clearly taken note.

## Decision 2028

Enough with the parsing of words. What do the people showing up at a midweek rally with the vice president think about Vance as the next Republican presidential nominee?

In Michigan, Lynda Nixon of Dearborn Heights and Sarah Marvel of Dearborn told me they would love for Vance to run for president in 2028. Marvel, wearing a brand-new T-shirt bearing an AI-generated image of Vance that she purchased while waiting in the security line, said she would prefer the vice president run with Marco Rubio as his running mate. When I asked the two friends if they think Vance, Rubio, and anyone else who wants to run for president should battle it out in a competitive primary, Nixon said she thinks Trump should make the decision on which one should be the nominee.

But Bob Doyle, who drove down to Howell from his home in Genesee County, said that he likes both Vance and Rubio and wants to see some competition. “I hope President Trump does say there should be a primary,” Doyle told me.

Vance would enter the 2028 fray with lots of name recognition, and the midterm campaigning is giving him lots of experience on the trail. Still, you can tell this would-be nominee still has some campaigning kinks to smooth out.

In Montana, Vance breezed right past his own mispronunciation of the state capital, Helena. (He put the emphasis on the middle syllable and pronounced it “lean.”) The crowd murmured, and Vance, clearly picking up on the disturbance, repeated the incorrect pronunciation. Quite a few people were looking at each other, shaking their heads in disbelief at the way this out-of-state politician had screwed up. Finally, the crowd erupted with a cry of “HELENA” (like “Helen” with an “uh” at the end). Vance laughed off the mistake.

“I’m humble enough to admit when I’m wrong,” Vance said.

## Poll of the Week

Here’s a marker that’s (somewhat) in Vance’s favor if he hopes to eventually strike out on his own politically: He’s not as unpopular as Trump! That’s according to the latest data from the Morning Consult’s weekly tracking poll of registered voters, which finds the current president’s approval/disapproval rating at 41 and 56 percent, a pretty dismal showing and the lowest for Trump in this poll since his presidency began.

Trump’s vice president, meanwhile, has a 44 percent approval rating and a 48 percent disapproval rating. Is this great for Vance? No, he’d of course want to be above water. But it suggests that there’s at least some separation in the minds of some voters between these two Republicans, which will be a necessary, if not sufficient, condition for Vance to win a general election.

But the Morning Consult poll does show there’s hope for vice presidents attached to unpopular presidents, at some point down the line. Kamala Harris has emerged two years after her defeat in the last presidential election with … an even approval differential, at 47 percent.

## This Week in Zombie Reaganism

That Ronald Reagan is so hot right now.

And while that’s been the case for Republicans and conservatives since before the Gipper was elected president, here in the late Trump era I’ve been clocking something like strange new respect for Reagan from Democrats. Specifically, it’s Gen-X Democrats who have far fewer hang-ups than their boomer predecessors about invoking Reagan, especially if it’s just a chance to ding the current GOP leader.

In the Democratic response to Trump’s address to Congress in March 2025, Sen. Elissa Slotkin said that Reagan “must be rolling in his grave” over the treatment Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky had recently received in the Oval Office. And California Gov. Gavin Newsom not only quoted Reagan in a social media post late last year, he proclaims each February 6 “Ronald Reagan Day” in the Golden State. (Okay, so he’s required by state law to do so, but that’s a pesky detail.)

So I shouldn’t have been surprised to hear the 40th president invoked again from Seth Bodnar, a Montana Senate candidate. Technically, Bodnar’s no longer a Democrat, running as an independent in a crowded field for an open Senate seat. But Bodnar has the support of much of the Democratic establishment in Montana and nationally.

Speaking at a small town hall meeting in western Montana’s Deer Lodge this week, Bodnar mentioned some of Reagan’s final public remarks as president, made when Reagan awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom to his secretary of state, George Shultz, and the former Senate majority leader Mike Mansfield (of Montana, of course).

Bodnar noted that Reagan commented on America’s heritage as a nation of immigrants, citing his statement that “anyone, from any corner of the Earth, can come to live in America and become an American.” Later, he repeated Slotkin’s line about Reagan (along with Dwight Eisenhower) “rolling over in their graves” at Trump’s foreign policy. The older crowd nodded their heads.

## Spin the Black Circle

I don’t think I’ve ever grown tired of Gillian Welch’s *Soul Journey*. I can listen to it start to finish multiple times in a row, and I rarely go longer than a couple of weeks without giving it a spin (or whatever the streaming digital equivalent is). On this tight, 10-track album Welch and her partner, David Rawlings, pitch back and forth between the brooding folk-rock of tunes like “One Monkey” and “Wayside / Back in Time” and the sparse acoustic arrangements on “Make Me a Pallet on Your Floor” and “One Little Song.”

All the songs here are driven by Welch’s breathy contralto, which conveys both a world-weariness and optimism matched by her frequently biographical lyrics, including on “No One Knows My Name” and “Wrecking Ball.” Even after countless listens, it still feels like the same musical soul journey, no more so than on the transcendent “I Made a Lover’s Prayer” and the climactic crescendo of its final harmonica solo.
