# Meet the Post-Religious Right

*A review of Scott Greer's “Whitepill,” a new history of the online right.*

Scott Greer is a relatively prominent dissident right figure. He’s been described to me as the “MAGA Whisperer,” someone who explains the MAGA base and its moods to many Republicans in Washington.

He was also a part of the original alt-right movement from early 2010s, having worked as an editor at Richard Spencer’s Radix Journal. He as a new book out from the dissident right publishing outfit Passage Press called *Whitepill: The Online Right and the Making of Trump’s America*. It’s the first insider and sympathetic account of this movement.

I review the book in the new November issue of First Things magazine, in which I note that the online right as we know it today is a manifestation of the post-religious right that Ross Douthat once warned about.

> Reading Whitepill reminded me of Ross Douthat’s quip: “If you dislike the religious right, wait till you meet the post-religious right.” The people involved in the online right are overwhelmingly secular, with a few exceptions like the Catholic Nick Fuentes. Some of them, like BAP, are explicitly anti-Christian. Alt-right intellectual influences coming from the Nouvelle Droite are often explicitly anti-Christian as well. The few Christians who were part of the broader alt-right ecosystem—Greer labels them “trads”—tended to be very traditionalist Catholics or Eastern Orthodox who were far outside the mainstream of American Christianity.

Greer himself appears irreligious. I didn’t go into this in the review, but Greer is very vague about the trads and goes into little detail about them, despite that group being the people who were responsible for doxxing him.

But there are so many of these different groups, and so many weird happenings over the past 15 years that it’s almost impossible to discuss them all in any detail. The book is overflowing with names most people have never heard of as it is.

Greer’s book tells two parallel, intertwining stories. One is the history of the Trump era. The other is the history of the online right. When it comes to Trump, Greer focuses on identity issues as core to his appeal:

> What’s different in Greer’s account is that he is especially keen to convey the centrality of identity issues to the Trump movement. Many observers have used an economic and social lens to explain Trumpism. They see his appeal lying primarily in material factors such as the disappearance of well-paying blue-collar work, left-behind communities, and the opioid crisis. His voters’ resentment is thus understood as coming from being left behind as a class by successful elites. This is the implicit framing in JD Vance’s Hillbilly Elegy, the work of Chris Arnade and his division of the world into “front row” and “back row” people, and Oren Cass’s American Compass project. It leads to politics that seek to mobilize a “multi-racial working class.”
>
> Greer rejects this framing. He thinks it is a convenient, politically correct fiction, one promoted by people who want to direct Trumpist energy in their own preferred direction. Instead, he argues that the appeal of Trump comes from identity issues: American identity, but also white identity.

In keeping with this theme, his online right history focuses on the alt-right properly so-called, the particular movement that emerged around Richard Spencer. This makes sense for Greer, who was once part of it, and thus wants to tell the insider story, as well as the ways his own positions have evolved over the years. (He tells that story more specifically in a Substack post on why he is not a white nationalist).

I personally found his account too alt-right centric. In my view there were three founding populations of the online right: the alt-right, the neoreactionaries, and manosphere. I’m probably biased myself in how I see the world here, but in my view the manosphere (which you can also think of as the “red pill” movement) is the most important founding group to the online right:

> Greer also gives little attention to the manosphere, which was arguably the most important founding population of the online right. The manosphere was a collection of blogs and forums that grew out of the seduction (pickup artist) community about the same time as the alt-right and neo-reaction. This group was politicized as a result of controversy called Gamergate, an episode too baroque to explain here. The Gamergaters were largely male video gamers who didn’t appreciate feminists and left-wing journalists trying to mess with their subculture. Early progenitors of what Greer calls “FanDuel Americans,” they aren’t really conservatives in any conventional sense; they simply want leftist scolds to leave them alone to enjoy their sports, pot, porn, and video games. This manosphere-Gamergater-influenced ethos underlies a huge ecosystem of podcasters and influencers, and it is the dominant component of the online right and beyond. The manosphere and related groups—which are notably racially diverse—don’t care much about the identity issues that matter to Greer

I have a lot more to say about *Whitepill* in my full review in First Things.
