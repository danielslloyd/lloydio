# The Whipsaw Right

*Republican politicians think they know what their base believes. Flock cameras show it might believe the opposite next month.*

Law and order has long been a core tenet of the American right. Yet today it is Republican politicians themselves who are getting rid of a piece of police technology - at the behest of their own base.

In late August, Texas Gov. Greg Abbott paused state funding for Flock cameras. About the same time, Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis ordered the removal of Flock cameras from state highways there. Local governments around the country, even in Republican areas, are terminating contracts for the cameras.

This sudden eruption of bipartisan opposition to Flock cameras - which automatically sense and record the license plate numbers of passing cars - has roiled state and local politics.

The left opposes Flock cameras because they’ve been used for assisting ICE in immigration enforcement. Thus the populist right here has turned not just against its traditional law and order approach, but also against a key tool that Trump has been using to fulfill his core campaign promise to crack down on illegal immigration.

This shows how, as its online influencers and politicians have become more unmoored, the online right has become a chaotic mass that can whipsaw politics and drive Republican party policy in the absence of any direction from Trump - and maybe even in opposition to him.

I’ve argued that right populism is fading as a political force in America. Post-Trump, it seems likely to retreat to the minority position it always held within the GOP. But populism as a governing regime and populism as a chaotic force are different things. The latter could easily continue to whipsaw our politics in unpredictable ways if it alights on a topic that catches fire. And as we’re seeing with Flock cameras and data centers, when that happens, elected officials are forced to respond.

Republican anti-Flock sentiment seems to have originated with the fringe candidate for Florida governor, James Fishback. Fishback is a great example of the kinds of candidates that have been emerging from the populist milieu. He made many controversial statements, for example saying a “government whistleblower” told him that his frontrunner opponent was being investigated for sex trafficking.

Opposition to Flock cameras was one of the central pillars of Fishback’s campaign. He held a “Block Flock Now” rally in the state, and created a podium with that slogan out of a music stand with a sign draped over it. This was so popular that eventual primary winner Byron Donalds was forced to call for a pause on Flock contracts.

Fishback was trounced in the primary, garnering only 10% of the vote. But his anti-Flock polemics outlived his candidacy and rapidly conquered the populist right nationwide after a Washington Post investigation of police abuse of Flock data raised the issue’s visibility. It’s a great example of how an idea can go from the fringes to the mainstream in a shockingly quick span of time.

Why does the populist right fear Flock cameras? They seem to have become something of a condensed symbol of fears over the surveillance state (and, as with the similar anti-data center movement, artificial intelligence). Some libertarian types were long opposed to Flock, and stoked fears that it would lead to things like surreptitious gun registries created by tracking cars going to gun stores. (The DEA did previously evaluate using license plate readers to monitor gun shows back in the 2000s). A common new fear is that they will be used to enforce a new round of future pandemic lockdowns (notwithstanding that Flock cameras were already widely deployed during the recent Covid pandemic). The specific complaints appear emergent, arising from whatever goes most viral in the algorithm. It’s a collection of ideas arising bottom-up rather than policy development from the top down, one that takes place against a backdrop of dramatically lower trust in institutions in our society.

Flock cameras have inspired incredible rage. I sometimes get hit hard for my takes on X, but the outpouring of vitriol over supportive statements about Flock, including intimations of physical violence, is unlike anything I’ve experienced before.

Anti-Flock activism is also another example of the horseshoe theory in action. Anti-law enforcement and anti-ICE activists on the left, plus some traditional civil liberties groups have joined with the populist right and libertarian elements around a joint cause. Their motivations may differ but their objectives are the same. This is similar to what we’ve seen with opposition to data centers. Since many online right influencers and podcasters aren’t especially partisan, or certainly not GOP loyalists, this suggests more possible left-right populist alignments in the future.

The net result is that many places might lose a highly useful law enforcement tool. Where I live, Flock cameras have played a key role in catching criminals who come from other nearby places - or even from outside the country. For example, organized gangs of South American criminals have been robbing high-end homes in suburbs across America, including here. Flock cameras are essentially the only way to catch these thieves, who are often long gone and out of state by the time local cops get a report. In one recent case, Flock cameras caught four Colombians who stole over six figures worth of jewelry and bags from a nearby house. The crooks were arrested in next door Ohio.

There are legitimate concerns about these cameras. The use case for Flock is much more questionable in rural areas than suburban ones like mine. Police can abuse the cameras to stalk ex-girlfriends, etc., and there have been a number of cases of this around the country. Safeguards around Flock use definitely need to be in place. Charles Lehman and Rafael Mangual provide some sensible guidelines in a new Manhattan Institute paper.

With proper safeguards in place, we should retain Flock as a critical law enforcement tool. In an era where surveillance devices are ubiquitous, and the police can get access to the data on any private platform when needed, privacy fears specific to Flock are badly overstated.

What the Flock controversy shows is the way social media driven populism can lead to unpredictable eruptions of anger on unexpected issues that can whipsaw politics. Whatever politicians think their base believes, it might well think the exact opposite next month. They are operating on an unstable terrain.
